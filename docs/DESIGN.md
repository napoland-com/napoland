# napoland: game design

The decisions so far, with the reason for each. Change this file when a decision changes.

## The pitch

**Pacific Drive without the car, as an online game for phones.** You live in a cabin in Stonebrook, a small town at the edge of a strange, dark forest. You go out to gather what the forest gives (resources, power-ups), and you have to come back before your energy runs out. The farther you go, the more dangerous it gets and the better the finds. Alone you can explore the near woods; to go really far, you need other people.

## Pillars

1. **No fights, no PvP, no missions.** Tension comes from the world: distance, energy, weather, night, creatures that chase you, anomalies. Other players are only ever help.
2. **Leave home, come back.** Every trip is a decision: one more find, or turn back now. Trips last 5 to 15 minutes, which suits phones.
3. **Farther is harder and richer.** Difficulty and rewards grow with distance from home.
4. **Together you go farther.** Deep areas need several people (see Cooperation).
5. **One fixed world you learn.** The map does not reshuffle, so players can share guides, routes and strategies. It grows over time as new regions are added farther out.

## The story: NAPO and Napoland

**N.A.P.O., the National Anomalous Phenomena Observatory**, was originally an organization, or a research program, set up to study strange environmental phenomena. Something went wrong. The territory around it became unstable, dangerous, and in the end abandoned or quarantined. People started calling the whole affected region **Napoland**. Stonebrook named it first; NAPO's orders took the name up later, as the Napoland Zone. The game is set there, and the players are Napoland residents.

What happened, as far as anyone in Stonebrook can tell:

1. **The Old Stone.** Stonebrook was a logging town, named for the standing stone by its brook, until its sawmill shut for a winter and never opened again. The stone hummed on cold nights, and nobody minded.
2. **The Observatory.** Then the hum got into the radios and compasses started turning toward the woods, and NAPO came to study it. It bought the land down the south road, on the far side of town from the woods, and built a research station there, a tower to listen with (its dish turned north, to the woods), a bunker, and a checkpoint at the end of its grounds. Half the town worked for it: drivers, cooks, a lineman, a nurse. NAPO found that the hum was not only in the Old Stone: the rocks deep in the woods hummed too, the same hum, as if the stones were answering each other. It set a listening post by them, and carried some of them south to its field site to listen closer.
3. **The answer.** So NAPO answered. One night the Tower sent the hum back into the woods, and the woods replied: light came up between the trees, the first surge rolled out from deep in toward the town, and the Old Stone cracked. It has shed shards ever since, and the stones that hum back pull them in: shards turn up deep in the woods and at NAPO's field site, most of all while the woods are restless, and by the old poles in a storm, as if the hum ran down the wires.
4. **Napoland.** The Tower still pulses every 40 minutes, and the woods answer on the same clock: each surge starts at the deepest place and rolls toward town, and dies at the town's edge, so the south road beyond it stays calm. Every third night the sky goes green. And once a week, on the night of the week NAPO answered, the woods answer longest: the whole day is one night under the lights, what the hum pulls up comes back twice as fast, and the watchers never settle. It is the Long Night, and it is the town's: that night nobody tends the lodge's fire alone, not even Walt, who strung the wire the answer went down and sits up by that fire every time it comes round, so whoever is about brings resin and cloth to keep it going until dawn. The town holds that the woods remember whether it did. Things turn up that were not there the day before. Tall pale figures stand in the trees and move only while nobody looks at them (NAPO's notes call them observers; in Stonebrook they are watchers).
5. **The quarantine.** NAPO closed the south road at its checkpoint, drew a line on its maps around everything north of it, the Napoland Zone, and told everyone to leave. Most of Stonebrook drove down the south road, a street at a time, each waiting its turn on the verge by the road and leaving there what would not fit in the cars; NAPO said two weeks. Some of the cars got no farther than the stretch north of NAPO's station, and stand there still. Its crews kept coming in for a while, in white suits, and then they stopped. The station went quiet, nobody has kept the checkpoint for years, and nobody knows whether NAPO still exists.
6. **The residents.** Twenty-three people stayed, or came back (Stonebrook's sign still says "Pop. 23"; nobody has changed it since): the Napoland residents, and every player is one of them. They keep the fires going, bring home what glows and what NAPO left, and feed the shards back to the Old Stone, because when it has enough it wakes and the surges grow gentler, as if it were taking back what the answer broke off it.

- **Why:** it gives a reason for everything the world already shows (surges on a fixed clock, aurora nights, shards, strange objects, watchers, a town most people left), it gives the game its name, and what NAPO left behind makes landmarks in a world you learn by walking it.
- **Where things are**, one logic for the whole map: the woods get deeper to the north, toward the stones that hum back. The Near Woods come first, and every deeper region opens north beyond them ([deeper-regions](../roadmap/deeper-regions.md)). The South Road is the other way: calm, and it ends at NAPO's checkpoint on the quarantine line, the edge of the Napoland Zone (what lies past it is outside the Zone, and still open). The Old Stone is the other end of the hum, not a door. For now every area has its paper map, and the map you open is the one of the area you are in; farther out there will be areas without one, whose maps have to be found.
- **Who tells it**, each in their own words: **Mira** by the notice board (the woods, and how to come back from them); **Walt Pruitt** by the fire in Stonebrook Lodge, who kept the north line for the power company and then NAPO's (he strung its wire from the station to the Tower), went with the others when NAPO said two weeks, came back for his truck and stayed, and remembers the night of the answer; **Ruth** in the NAPO Bunker, NAPO's nurse, who stayed with the ones who would not leave and keeps the bunker's fire going; **Vera** in the NAPO Laboratory, the last of NAPO's researchers, who never left and is still listening. NAPO's own words are in its logs, radios, panels and warning signs, which you read like signs.
- **What NAPO and the people who left left behind** can appear throughout the map: old NAPO facilities, signs, antennas, laboratories, shelters, warning signs and abandoned equipment, and the houses, cars and belongings of those who went. **Built** (see [World structure](#world-structure)): on the South Road, the research station (its laboratory, dormitory and stores) and, across the road from it, NAPO's motor pool (its box trucks and fuel pump, fenced), the NAPO Tower and its shed, the NAPO Bunker, the field site with its sample cages (steel mesh around rocks NAPO carried from deep in the woods, each with its tag) and the checkpoint on the quarantine line, with NAPO's power line, warning signs, logs and crates of equipment; and the jam, where the line of leavers stopped (their cars on both shoulders, doors open, a trunk up, their luggage between). In the Near Woods, NAPO's Zone warning where the road comes in, one of its jeeps burned out off the road across from the last light, its survey stakes at the foot of the rocks and its listening post by the ring of stones; and from the logging days, the log landing beside the turnout (stumps, and the log deck the last crew stacked) with its skid road climbing into the cut. In Stonebrook, NAPO's evacuation notice by the south road and, beside it, the verge where the town waited its turn, with what would not fit in the cars (suitcases, boxes, a rocking chair, a piano under a tarp, a child's bike, an empty birdcage, and a cardboard sign); the houses of four families who left (the Okadas, the Hales, the Dahls and the Lindqvists), dark behind their drawn curtains, each room as they left it with one thing in it to read; and the old sawmill on the brook where it runs into the pond, with the last logs and its logging truck. The lamp in the Near Woods that no wires run to may be NAPO's too. Still to come: more of it farther out ([napo-in-the-world](../roadmap/napo-in-the-world.md)).
- **Names in the world** come from it, for example: NAPO Research Station, NAPO Tower, NAPO Laboratory, NAPO Equipment, NAPO Bunker, Napoland Zone, Napoland Residents; the answer (the night it went wrong), observers (NAPO's word for the watchers), the quarantine line (where the checkpoint closed the road), the leavers' camp (where the people who left spent their last night), the verge (where the town waited its turn to leave), the jam (where the line of leavers stopped).
- **Told by the world, never by a mission** (pillar 1): by what stands out there, what signs and NAPO's logs say, what people tell you and what items are.
- **Figures you see:** the figures of people (the echo of someone's last steps) are their size and see-through, and walk while you watch; the observers are tall, solid and pale-faced, and move only while nobody watches. Nothing else in the world looks like either, so nobody takes one for the other.
- **Chapters: the story you go through.** Each player also goes through the story, chapter by chapter (`content/story.json`). Everyone starts in the first (Home), and every chapter after it is reached by one thing the game already asks of you: bring something home, walk into a place, feed a fire out in the wilds, give the Old Stone a shard, pick up a find, talk to someone or read one of NAPO's desks. So the first chapters teach the game (the stash and XP, fires and energy, fires that burn down unless someone feeds them), and the later ones take you down the South Road to what NAPO did. So far: Home; What glows; The old cabin; Whoever comes next; The lineman; The bunker; What to do during an event; The laboratory; The answer; What the answer broke; Do not switch off.
  - **Not a mission** (pillar 1 holds): nothing is asked, timed, rewarded or failed, there is no task list and no marker on the world, and ignoring the story costs nothing. People hint at what comes next in their own words: before what they always say, or after it when talking to them is what moves the story on. What they hint changes as your story moves on.
  - **The journal**, in the menu, keeps the chapters you reached, the latest first. A banner says when a new one is in it (once nobody is talking to you and no panel covers it), and a dot on the menu stays until you open the journal. It is the one book the game keeps for you: what you find out besides the story (field notes, the notes people left) goes into it too, never into a second book.
  - **What people say** is put together in one place, in one order: the hint of the chapter you are in comes first (or last, when talking to them is what moves the story on), then what they say once about something you did for the first time, then what they have to say about the day (Mira: what the woods are like today; Walt, on the Long Night: the lodge's fire), then what they always say. **Built** of what you did: Mira after your first collapse and after the first surge that catches you out there, and Walt after the first thing you make (A first goal, in The loop). Anything added later joins that same order.
  - **Never finished:** no percentage and no count of chapters. The latest chapter written is where the story rests until the next one is. New chapters are added at the end as the world grows (with new regions, say), and whoever reached the end reads on from there.
  - **Writing one:** add it at the end of `content/story.json` and bump its version; never remove, rename or reorder a chapter, since each player's place in the story is the id of the latest chapter they reached. `npm run validate` checks that the people, desks, maps and items a chapter names exist. What a chapter says is part of the story: it follows this section, and a new fact goes here first.
- **Still open:** what the stones are and what the hum says; what the observers are; why nobody switches the Tower off; whether NAPO still exists; where the south road goes past the checkpoint; who keeps the old cabin's fire going. A new fact about the story is a design decision: write it here first, so signs, people and items never contradict each other.

## The loop

1. Wake up at home, in your cabin, by the fire (**built**: the first time you play and after every collapse you wake up in front of your cabin's fire, facing the room; see [World structure](#world-structure)). Equip gear at the chest by the fire, and make or mend it at the workbench beside the chest. Choosing what to wear and what to carry is part of planning a trip: out there you can check what you wear and put on a piece you carry, but a carried piece takes a slot and weighs what it weighs (**built**: [gear-on-the-road](../roadmap/gear-on-the-road.md); the bag you wear changes only at home).
2. Walk out through the town into the wilds. Energy drains in the wilds, faster the farther you are, at night and in the rain; in town and inside buildings it holds. The only way to get it back is **a fireplace**. Fires in town (your home's, the lodge's) are tended and never go out (but for the lodge's on the Long Night, when the town keeps it going: Weather, below), and so is the fire of the shelter nearest to town in each region next to it (the Near Woods' old cabin, the South Road's NAPO Bunker), so a new player always has one safe fire whichever way they go. **The other fires out in the wilds burn down**: a well-fed one gives energy back at the full rate, a low one (under 3 minutes of fuel) only glows and gives 40%, a dead one gives nothing. Anyone can feed one (A, facing it; it asks how many) with what burns: resin (5 minutes), cloth (1.5); a fire holds 30 at most. So players keep the shelters going for each other without ever meeting, and whoever arrives cold at a dead fire had better carry resin. Tuned so that standing at the edge of the Near Woods in the rain, dry and light, empties a full bar in about 5.5 minutes, and its deepest corner in under 2.
   - **What else wears you down out there** (each a factor on the drain): a **heavy bag** (items weigh something; at 10 kg or more the drain is 40% faster, so every find is a choice), being **wet** (rain soaks you through in 2.5 minutes, anywhere outdoors; soaked drains 50% faster; a burning fire dries you in 25 seconds, a roof slowly), a **surge** (below), and a **hitchhiker**: at night, 25 steps or more from home and away from light, something may cling to your back (50% faster) until you reach a street light, a burning fire, a roof or light a flare.
3. Gather resources and power-ups. What you pick up goes in your bag (limited slots). Finds are shared: when someone takes one, a new one of the same kind grows later somewhere else in the same kind of place (deep finds stay deep).
4. Come home and stash what you carry. Stashing earns XP; levels and home upgrades let you go farther next time.
   - **Built:** the stash is the chest by the fire at home (A, facing it); each player sees only their own things in it. Everything put in earns its item's XP (`xp` in `content/items.json`: a glowcap 1, resin 2, scrap 3, a shard 12, a strange object 15, a charm 25...), once: what you take out and bring back earns nothing again, and what you use up after taking it out (a thermos drunk, resin burned) no longer counts against new finds, so XP cannot be farmed. Gear counts the same way: a piece put on at the chest counts as taken out, and it comes home whether it is put away from the bag or taken off at the chest (a piece that never was home, a strange object's, earns its XP then, the first time). What a strange object taken out of the stash turns into, a piece of gear too, counts as taken out in its place, so it earns nothing when it comes home either (one found out there turns into something that earns its XP). Reaching level L takes 30 × (L − 1)² XP in all (level 2 at 30, 5 at 480, 10 at 2,430, 20, the top, at 10,830), and every level makes the energy bar 5 bigger (100 at level 1, 195 at 20). Levels 5, 10, 15 and 20 each open an outfit too, for others to see ([Look and feel](#look-and-feel)). Level 20 stays the top, but stashing keeps counting XP past it: every 1,500 XP more earns a merit, which buys a look for the wardrobe (Look and feel, past level 20). Home upgrades are still to come, in the cabin that is your own ([cabin-comfort](../roadmap/cabin-comfort.md)).
5. **If your energy runs out** you wake up at home, by the fire in your cabin. What you carried falls out where you collapsed, as a small pile on the ground (the bag itself is equipment and stays with you, like all you wear; a piece of gear you carried falls in as it is, with its condition, quirk and level). If you go back for it, you get everything. If someone else finds it first, they get a random half and the rest is lost. The pile disappears one hour after you collapsed, and a new collapse replaces your old pile: each player has at most one.

**Coming back** ([daily-parcels](../roadmap/daily-parcels.md)). The residents look after whoever comes back. The first time you sign in, a welcome parcel waits in your chest; after that, the first time you play each calendar day (UTC), signed in, the day's parcel goes into your chest, never into the bag. The parcels follow a calendar of seven days, Monday to Sunday (the week of the weekly conditions), the same for everyone and pinned on the notice board; play all seven and Sunday's parcel also holds a NAPO lockbox. Parcels earn no stash XP (they were not brought home), and missing a day costs only that week's lockbox: nothing is ever taken away. They come from the town's own stores, shared out, since nothing comes into the Zone from outside any more. It asks nothing of you but coming back, so it is not a mission (pillar 1).
**Built:** the welcome parcel is 5 resin, 4 cloth, a thermos and 2 road flares. It comes the first time you play signed in (never as a guest: a guest who signs in, and so keeps the character, gets it then; players who signed in before there were parcels get theirs the next time they play), and it stands for that first day, which counts as a day you came back. Then the day's parcel comes the first time you play signed in on each calendar day, or at midnight UTC if you are playing then: Monday 3 resin and 2 cloth, Tuesday a thermos and 2 scrap, Wednesday 2 road flares and 2 cloth, Thursday 3 resin and 2 wire, Friday a thermos and 3 cloth, Saturday 2 scrap, 2 wire and a road flare, Sunday 4 resin and a thermos, and a NAPO lockbox for whoever came back on all seven days of that week (`parcels` in `content/items.json`). A banner says so as it comes ("A parcel waits in your chest", and what is in it; the welcome parcel's also names what signing in put in your wardrobe, "and a NAPO work suit in your wardrobe"); the first time the chest opens after (or while it is open), a card at the top of the stash says what came, once ("Tuesday's parcel: a thermos, 2 scrap"). The notice board lists this week's calendar with today marked, and the days you came back this week ("You came back Mon, Tue, Thu.", or "You came home today." on your first day this week, and on your very first), with whether Sunday's lockbox is still yours this week or waits for the next; a guest sees the calendar and "Sign in to get the parcels." Taking what came out of the stash and putting it back earns nothing, as for anything taken out of the stash. A server without sign-in gives none.

**Rested while away** ([well-rested](../roadmap/well-rested.md)). Time away from the game fills a cup of rest, and while it holds anything, what you stash earns double: the extra comes out of the cup. It rewards time away, so skipping days costs nothing here, where the parcels reward coming back often; neither ever takes anything away, and neither asks anything of you (pillar 1).
**Built:** one XP of rest for every 20 minutes you are not playing, counted as you arrive from when you last played, up to three days' worth (216 XP); guests rest too. While the cup holds any, the XP stashing earns comes twice, the second time out of the cup and never more than it holds: only XP from bringing things home (a piece of gear that was never home counts when it comes off at the chest), never a parcel, a lockbox or anything else that earns nothing, such as what comes back after being taken out or what came out of a crate. The status panel shows the cup under your level ("Rested: 140 XP of doubled stashing left", with a bar of how full it is; empty, what fills it), the chest says it at the top of the stash while it holds any ("Rested: your next 140 XP from the chest count double."), and the XP that floats over your head as you stash says the doubled part ("+24 XP (12 rested)"). Arriving after three hours or more away with rest in the cup, a banner says so once the name of the place has been read ("Rested" and "Your next 140 XP from the chest count double."). The cup is kept with your character.

**A first goal on the first day** ([first-day](../roadmap/first-day.md)). A new player always sees one thing worth going for, and nothing is asked, timed or failed (pillar 1). **Built:** the bag (under what you carry) and the top of the stash say what gear comes next: of what the workbench makes that you own none of yet (worn, carried or in the stash), one the stash can pay for now ("You can make rubber gloves at the workbench beside the chest."), or else the one that lacks the fewest, counting what the stash and the bag hold ("Next: rubber gloves. 1 more resin.": the welcome parcel's 5 resin and 4 cloth leave them one resin short), or to put away what you carry when that is all it lacks; nothing once you own all of it. Standing at the workbench, a tap on it opens that recipe's card there; anywhere else it is only words. The first 40 steps into the Near Woods hold more resin and glowcaps (3 more resin on the grass and 4 more glowcaps in the damp, that grow back sooner), so a first trip never comes home empty; the finds deeper in are as they were. The story already points the first steps (chapter 1's hint from Mira, and chapter 2's banner at the first stash); after that, Mira says something once after your first collapse (what you carried lies where you fell for an hour) and after the first surge that catches you out there, and Walt, in the lodge, after the first thing you make: each the first time you talk to them after, and not again (`remarks` in `content/story.json`). For it the game counts gear made, collapses and surges that caught you out in the wilds.

**Light is not energy.** Street lights, and later torches and gadgets, let you see where you walk; some areas will be almost dark without them. They do not refill energy, but they shelter you from a surge and shake off a hitchhiker.

**Every building can be entered**, like in FireRed: walk into the door and you are inside a small room (a map of its own). Shelters and some houses have a fireplace; empty houses are dark. Behind your home's door is your own cabin, where nobody else ever is.

## World structure

| Layer | Who shares it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Your cabin | Only you (and guests you invite later) | Upgradeable. Holds your chest (your stash, your gear and the wardrobe) and the workbench. |
| Your street | About 30 neighbors | Streets fill up; new streets open as players join. Friends can live on the same street. |
| Town square (Stonebrook) | Everyone | The notice board, the lodge, the road out. Splits into copies of ~150 players when crowded, keeping friends together. |
| The wilds | Everyone | One fixed map split into regions. A region holds a few hundred players; only an overcrowded region gets extra copies. The game remembers which copy a pile is in. |

**Built** ([own-cabin](../roadmap/own-cabin.md)): your cabin is Home, your house in Stonebrook, and it is yours alone. Everyone who walks in through that door is in a cabin of their own (the server keeps a copy of the room for each player), so nobody else is ever in yours; guests you invite come later, with [streets](../roadmap/streets.md). You wake up in it, in front of the fire and facing the room: the first time you play, after every collapse, and when the place you were last in is gone from the world. Its chest (your stash, as it always was) and the workbench beside it work as before, and stashing there moves the story on as ever. The door out leads to the street in front of the house, among everyone. Upgrades are still to come ([cabin-comfort](../roadmap/cabin-comfort.md)).

Everything in the world is shared (the ground, piles, weather, time of day); only your cabin, inventory and personal items are private.

Regions are separate maps joined at their edges, like FireRed's towns and routes: walking off the end of a road fades to the next map, which greets you with its name. So far: **Stonebrook** (the town); up its north road, **the Near Woods** (depth 1); and down its south road, **the South Road** (depth 1), NAPO's old grounds. Deeper regions will open to the north, beyond the Near Woods; the South Road ends at the quarantine line.

**Stonebrook** is home, and safe: nothing drains you in town. Home, where you wake, and the lot with Mira and the notice board; the lodge, where Walt sits by the fire; the Old Stone north of the main street. Most of the houses are dark: the empty house next to home, and the houses of four families who left when NAPO said two weeks (the Okadas' and, under a street light that still comes on, the Hales' across the main street from home; the Dahls' and the Lindqvists' on the old road to the mill), their curtains drawn and never lit, their names on the mailboxes by their doors. Inside, each is as it was left: cold, the furniture under dust sheets, what they packed and what they did not, and one thing to read that says a little of the night their street's turn came (a note on the table, a calendar, a child's drawing of the lights in the woods, a list of what to take). At the end of that road the old sawmill stands on the brook where it runs out of the woods into the pond, its last logs stacked and its logging truck rusting by its sign; its floor can be walked, dark, the saw still standing. By the south road, beside NAPO's notice, is the verge where the town waited its turn to leave, and what would not fit in the cars is there yet. The four houses hold a little cloth, the mill a little scrap. Its paper map was drawn by someone who stayed.

**The Near Woods**, up the north road, are the first of the wilds and the way deeper: the surges, storms and flashes, the watchers and the skulkers, the tall grass. The old road comes in past the last street light and NAPO's Zone warning, and across from the light one of NAPO's jeeps lies burned out in the firs it burned with; beside the gravel turnout, where the logging trucks turned, is the log landing, a clearing of stumps and the log deck the last crew stacked, with a skid road climbing out of it into the cut. Past the old car the track fords the creek to the crossroads; west lies the pond, east the old cabin (the safe fire), north the rocks, with NAPO's survey stakes at their foot, then the lonely light and the ranger's hut, and deepest the ring of stones, where NAPO listened, and the cabin at the end.

**The South Road** is the other way out of town, and the calmer one: the surges roll from deep in the woods toward town and die at its edge, so none reach the road; no storms or flashes either, nothing watches from its trees and nothing lies in wait in its ferns, but it runs a long way (the checkpoint is about 90 steps from town), so its danger is the distance. North to south: NAPO's first warning sign; the NAPO Bunker, where Ruth keeps the fire going (the road's safe fire, like the old cabin's in the woods), and west of the road the camp the leavers made, with a fire in the open; the jam, where the line of leavers stopped and never moved again, their cars nose to tail on both shoulders, doors open, a trunk up, their luggage between; the NAPO Research Station in its fenced yard (the NAPO Laboratory, where Vera is, the dormitory and the stores), and across the road NAPO's motor pool, its box trucks and fuel pump in a fenced lot; the NAPO Tower in its fence, with its shed and the panel that works it; a bog to the west (the sinks) and NAPO's field site to the east, with its sample cages, where shards and strange objects turn up; and at the end the checkpoint on the quarantine line, where a barrier and a truck close the road. Its finds lean on what people left: scrap and flares by the cars, cloth and flares in the stores, copper wire along NAPO's power line and by the masts. Like every area so far, it has its paper map.

## Movement and controls

- **Tile by tile** in four directions, like Pokemon FireRed, at **one speed** (a steady run). No sprint button.
- **Controls:** a **joystick** bottom left (it picks one of four directions), **A** (pick up, talk, open) and **B** (bag, back) bottom right. No text labels on the buttons. **B held** is a call ([calls without words](#together-chat-and-friends)): a tap on B still does what it always did, and held 300 ms or more with no panel, text box or question open, a fan of three calls opens above it, away from A.
- Joystick: a quick flick in a new direction **turns in place**; holding walks; the direction you already face walks at once; changing direction while walking does not stop.
- Tapping the ground walks there along the shortest path. Tapping a person or a sign walks up to it and talks.
- Top right there are only the **menu button** and the **chat button** beside it. **No minimap and no position marker, ever:** you learn the world by walking it (and players share what they learn). The one map is an old **paper map drawn by hand**, a tool you own ([Items](#items)): it shows the region's roads, trails, cabins and landmarks but never where you are, so you work that out from what you see around you. **Built:** the bag's header has one map button (M on a keyboard) for every map you own, and it opens the map of the area you are in; indoors, of the area outside. For now every area has one, and everyone starts with all three: Stonebrook (drawn by someone who stayed), the Near Woods, and the South Road (traced by hand from NAPO's own map of its grounds). Like every tool, a map is yours for good: it takes no slot, weighs nothing and never falls into a pile. The map covers the screen: forest shaded, paths and clearings left bare, the tall grass hatched, the road, the water, the power line, NAPO's buildings, masts and fences, the cabins and the sawmill, the cars and trucks, log decks and stumps, NAPO's stakes, cages and pump, what was left on the verge, the way home and the places people call by name (the pond, the ring of stones, the long grass, the sinks, the quarantine line, the verge, the log landing, the jam) written in by hand, and "frozen in winter" by the water that freezes (Seasons), everything a little off where it really is. Tap the paper to see it full size and pan around; B (or M) puts it away. Farther out there will be areas without a map; there the button says so, until you find one.

## Creatures

Creatures do not fight. They chase. You are a little faster than them, so you escape by moving away in time. If one catches you, you lose energy and drop something. They do not follow you into town, under a street light or into tall grass.

The first kind are the **watchers** of the Near Woods (three, in its deeper half): tall figures with pale blank faces that come closer **only while nobody on the map looks their way** (faces them, with the four-way facing everyone already has). Face one and it freezes; a friend facing it holds it for you. Out of the light and out of tall grass, within 9 steps, it walks toward you (slower than you walk); reaching you, it takes 15 energy and one thing you carry, then goes away for a minute or two. They never stand in light, next to a fire, in tall grass or near a flare. On aurora nights they are faster. **Built:** someone carrying a live find glows, and watchers come for them from 12 steps instead of 9.

**Built:** the ones that chase are the **skulkers**: something long and low that lies in the ferns of the Near Woods, three of them, only 50 steps or more from home, and only at night (aurora nights too) or while a storm blows over the region; by day they are gone. Lying in wait, one hears you walking within 6 steps, or sees you standing still within 3, unless you are in a street light, by a burning fire, near a flare or in tall grass. Then it comes, a quarter slower than you walk: the ferns thrash where it runs, a sharp cry sounds the moment it goes after you ("Something is after you. Run"), and a rustling that grows as it nears. Walk away in time and you get out. It gives up after 20 seconds, or when you reach a street light, a burning fire's warmth, tall grass, the way out of the region or the edge of its range (it never goes nearer home than 50 steps), and goes back to its lair; it never steps into light, next to a fire, into tall grass or onto an exit, and there are none in town or inside. Reaching you, it costs 20 energy and one bag slot, which falls out as your pile where you stand (so you can go back for it: it replaces an older pile of yours, as a collapse does), then it is gone for a minute or two. A flare sends it off, like a watcher. Where they live and when is data per region (`skulkers` in the map).

**Tall grass hides you.** **Built:** eight patches of knee-high grass in the Near Woods that you wade into at your usual pace and crouch in, only your head and shoulders showing. Seven lie in its deeper half, beside the trails a chased player runs along: north of the pond where the west trail comes down from the fern hollow (the long grass) and south of it on the back way, on the old cabin's west side, at both feet of the rocks (the deer beds, north), on the rim of the ring of stones' glade and west of the cabin at the end; one lies shallow, in the headlight clearing, where a new player finds out what it is before it matters. Creatures never step into tall grass, a chase ends when you reach it, and nothing notices you while you are in it (a watcher you face still freezes: looking is what holds it). It hides you from creatures only: you still tire and get wet there, a hitchhiker can still cling to you, and a surge, a storm or a flash still finds you. The deep ferns are the other way round: they hide the skulkers. The paper maps show the tall grass as a hatch, with a key in the margin, so a route can run from patch to patch.

## Cooperation (how groups go farther)

Built so far, working even when nobody is online at the same time:

- **Shelter fires** (all but the nearest one) burn down and anyone can feed them: you arrive at a fire someone else kept going.
- **Marks**: crush a glowcap (Use in the bag) to paint a glowing arrow on the ground where you stand, pointing where you face, in your jacket color. Everyone sees it for a day (a good neighbor's for longer, up to a week: Status); each player keeps their newest 6. Routes spread without a map.
- **Thanks** ([thanks](../roadmap/thanks.md)): fires and arrows let players help each other without meeting, and a thanks lets the helper know it did something, with no words and no XP (Death Stranding's likes). **Built:** a fire out in the wilds remembers the last 3 players who fed it (in memory, like its fuel), and an arrow knows its painter. Warm a few seconds at a burning fire someone else fed, standing still, or stop on the tile an arrow points to after stepping off it the way it points, and the text box offers once to thank them, by name: "Ana fed this fire. Thank Ana?", "Ana painted this arrow. Thank Ana?" (at a fire, the latest of its feeders you may thank). Never for yourself, for someone you thanked that UTC day or block, never while a question, a panel (or a card in one), the fan of calls or someone's lines are up (it waits, and is dropped if you walked away meanwhile), and never again in that session for a fire or an arrow you said no to. Walking straight on past an arrow never stops you. YES says "You thank Ana for feeding the fire." Taking something someone left in a crate thanks them too (below). Each player thanks each helper once a UTC day, and guests give and get thanks too: they carry no words. A thanks gives the helper 3 energy if they are out in the wilds right then (a shelter out there counts), 5 such gifts a trip at most (a trip ends when they walk into their home room, or collapse), with a float over their head: "Tess thanked you: +3". Anywhere else a line in their text box says it all: "Tess thanked you for the fire at the ranger's hut." The rest waits for the letter: when the helper next walks into their home room (or wakes up there after a collapse, or comes back into the game there), the text box reads out who thanked them while they were away, and for what, grouped, the most thanked first, in three short lines at most: "While you were away, 4 people thanked you for the fire at the ranger's hut." A fire goes by its room or its own name, an arrow by the nearest place the map names ("your arrow by the pond"). Someone who blocks you hears no thanks from you, and you are not told. Thanks received count toward the Good neighbor feat (Status). Who thanked whom, when and for what is kept 7 days, then deleted.
- **The notice board** in Stonebrook, next to Mira: the day and each region's rain and when it changes, the season and how long is left of it, what the woods are like today and this week (and next week's), each region's surge clock, which shelter fires are low or out, the collapses of the last hour, the Old Stone, and this week's parcels (Coming back).
- **The Old Stone** wakes when the whole server has fed it 20 shards (A, facing it). Awake, it halves the extra drain of every surge, and burns a shard every 30 minutes; at none left it sleeps, and the count starts again. It is kept across restarts. It is the other end of the hum, not a way anywhere: new regions open north, beyond the Near Woods (see [the story](#the-story-napo-and-napoland)).
- **A crate for whoever comes next** ([shelter-caches](../roadmap/shelter-caches.md)): a new player who reaches a dead fire with no resin is saved by someone who passed hours before, and a high-level player has a use for what they carry spare (Death Stranding's shared lockers). **Built:** every place out there where people rest by a fire has one: the three shelters of the Near Woods (the old cabin, the ranger's hut, the cabin at the end) and on the South Road the NAPO Bunker, the laboratory, the dormitory, the checkpoint and the leavers' camp, in the open at the edge of the clearing, three steps from its fire. In NAPO's rooms it is one of NAPO's supply crates, olive with its yellow stencil; anywhere else plain weathered boards with a chalk mark. Anyone opens it (A, facing it) into a panel like the chest's: it holds 6 things at most (one unit each), each saying who left it and when ("Fir resin, left by Ana, 2 h ago"), and your bag below. Each visit (from walking into its room, or coming within 3 tiles of the camp's crate, until you leave) you may leave one thing from your bag and take one out. Leaving asks first ("Leave 1 resin in the crate for whoever comes next?") and says what it did; gear stays out (it carries its own condition and quirk; tools are never in the bag), a live find left there goes dim, as in a pile, and a full crate says so. Taking asks nothing, and thanks whoever left it (Thanks: it counts for their Good neighbor, and their letter says "for the resin you left in the old cabin's crate"); what you left yourself you may take back, thanking nobody. What comes out of a crate earns no stash XP: it counts as taken out of your stash, like taking from your own chest, so crates cannot be farmed. An empty crate says "Nothing in it yet. Leave something for whoever comes next." What lies in the crates is kept across restarts, until someone takes it.
- **Echoes**: a pile keeps the last 16 steps its owner walked out there; near it, a see-through figure walks them again and sinks where they fell. You see where it went wrong.

Ideas to build and test:

- **Shared light:** deep areas drain energy fast unless you stand in someone's lantern light; more lanterns mean a bigger safe area.
- **Group gates:** some passages open only with several people pulling at once.
- **Rescue:** friends can carry you home when you collapse near them.
- **Heavy finds:** the best finds need two people to carry.
- **Camps:** a group can build a shared camp that works as a temporary home out in the woods.
- Solo players must still progress: better gear lets them go a bit deeper, just more slowly.

## Weather, seasons, day and night

Each region has its own climate (rainforest, snowy ridge, marsh, burnt forest...) and its own weather cycle; the seasons, and day and night, are the whole world's. Weather changes what you find (glowcaps after rain, shards at night, frozen lakes you can cross in winter) and how dangerous it is. The server owns all of it, so everyone sees the same sky.

Day and night are one for the whole world (one sun): a day is 48 minutes long, set by the wall clock (so a restart never jumps the sky, and players can learn it), 32 minutes of daylight from dawn, then 16 of night. **Every third night is an aurora**: green light, the dead power lines hum and glow, copper wire turns up by the poles (finds that grow only then), watchers are restless. Once a week a whole day is one: the Long Night (below). Only rain soaks you, so only rain falls on screen, and the night is dry everywhere.

**Weather per region** ([regional-weather](../roadmap/regional-weather.md)). Rain is each region's own: every outdoor map says when in the day it rains (its rain windows, counted from dawn, as data in the map), always on the wall clock, so everyone sees the same sky over the same place and learns it; a map that says nothing rains on the usual cycle. Choosing where to go is part of a trip. **Built:** Stonebrook and the Near Woods rain from 12 to 24 minutes after dawn (12 minutes, as before). The South Road, the calmer way, is the drier one too: its rain comes as the Near Woods' stops, from 24 to 30 minutes after dawn (6 minutes, half as long), so from the first rain of the day to the last one of the two roads out of town is always dry. A room hears the weather of the map its door opens onto. The drain, getting wet, the steps that count for the rain walker, the sound of rain, the sky you see and the finds that follow the weather all go by the weather of the region you are in. The notice board starts with the day (Night falls in about 18 minutes. / Night: no rain anywhere. Dawn in about 8 minutes.), then tells each region out there apart: The Near Woods: rain for about 10 minutes more. The South Road: dry for about 10 minutes, then rain. (or dry until nightfall). A region's own weather is also its storms (Hazards below).

**Seasons** ([seasons](../roadmap/seasons.md)). Seasons follow the wall clock too: each lasts one week, from Monday 00:00 UTC (the week of the conditions), in the order spring, summer, autumn, winter, then again, the same for everyone. What each changes is kept small, so it can be learned. **Built:**
- **Spring:** rain lasts half as long again, from where each region's starts (the Near Woods and Stonebrook 12 to 30 minutes after dawn, the South Road from 24 minutes until nightfall), and more glowcaps grow (6 more in the Near Woods, 4 more down the South Road).
- **Summer:** rain lasts half as long (the Near Woods 12 to 18 minutes after dawn, the South Road 24 to 27), and the dusk light lasts longer: night falls 36 minutes after dawn instead of 32, so the night is 12 minutes long.
- **Autumn:** more resin (5 more in the Near Woods, 3 more down the South Road), and storms twice as often: a region's storm round is half as long, moved on by a quarter of its usual round, so the Near Woods' storm, which came halfway between two surges, comes twice, ten minutes either side of where it was (7 to 10 and 27 to 30 minutes into each 40-minute round), still clear of the surges and the restless time (the maps are checked for it in every season).
- **Winter:** colder: every weather's cold drains a fifth more on top of what it does (0.2 more of the weather's extra drain, dry days too) and being wet chills half as much again, and cold resistance cuts all of it, so it matters more. The rain falls as snow (drawn as snow, quieter, and it wets you like rain). The ground, the trees and the rocks take a light frost. **The pond in the Near Woods and the brook in Stonebrook freeze** and can be crossed: their water is ice for the week, walked on like ground (the same rule on the server and in your game), the way home across it counts, and nothing grows or waits on it. When winter ends, whoever stands on the ice steps ashore onto the nearest ground, a pile lying on it washes up on the shore, and an arrow painted on it goes with the ice. The paper maps say "frozen in winter" by the pond and by the brook.
The whole world looks the season: a gentle grade on the ground, the grass, the ferns, the firs and the light (spring greener, summer warmer, autumn rust, winter frost). The notice board says the season, how long is left of it, what it changes and which comes next ("Winter, for about 4 days more: colder out there, and snow instead of rain, and the pond in the Near Woods and the brook in Stonebrook frozen hard enough to cross. Spring comes next."), and in winter its rain lines say snow; the status panel has a Season row ("Winter, 2 days left. Colder out there, and snow instead of rain."), and what drains you says the winter. As a season turns (a dawn), a banner says what it brings ("Winter" and what freezes; "Spring" and that the ice is gone).

**The Long Night** ([long-night](../roadmap/long-night.md)). Once a week the woods answer longest (the story, above), and the whole town has a date: nothing is asked of anyone, and a fire everyone shares either lasts the night or does not. **Built:** the game day that dawns on Saturday at 19:12 UTC (the 175th of the week's 210 game days, counted from Monday 00:00 UTC) is one night from its dawn to the next, at 20:00: 48 minutes of aurora instead of 16, over every region, in every season, with no rain and no dusk to wait for. The night before it is an aurora too (every third), so the lights come on at 18:56 UTC (19:00 in summer) and stay until 20:00. All of it is an aurora night as any other: the watchers are restless the whole time (as fast as on any aurora night), whatever comes out at night is out all of it, and copper turns up by the poles. **Its bonus:** wire and strange objects picked that night grow back twice as fast (`longNight` in `content/items.json`). **The lodge's fire:** that night nobody tends it, the one fire in town that ever goes out, and on that night alone. At 19:12 it has 15 minutes of fuel, half of what a fire holds, and it burns down like a shelter's (low, it only glows and gives 40%; out, nothing), so it cannot see the night through without the town, twice at least: 33 minutes more is the least that carries it to dawn, 7 resin or 22 cloth. Anyone feeds it as any fire (A, facing it), and whoever warms at it can thank whoever fed it. If it lasts until dawn, next week's Long Night keeps its bonus; if it goes out even once (lit again it warms, but it went out), next week's is only long and dark: the same aurora and the same restless watchers, and nothing grows back faster; and the week after has its bonus again if the fire lasted. The first Long Night a world sees has its bonus. The home fire, the old cabin's and the NAPO Bunker's stay tended, so a new player always has a safe fire. Whether it held is the whole world's, and it is kept across restarts, with the fire's fuel while the night is on; a server that was down at dawn counts the fire out if it had not the fuel to last. A banner says it begins ("The Long Night begins", with its bonus, or "Only long and dark this time") and how it ended ("Dawn. The lodge's fire held." or "Dawn. The lodge's fire went out."), each after the dawn's own banner. The notice board says all week when it comes and whether it keeps its bonus ("The Long Night comes in about 3 days: an aurora from dawn to dawn, and wire and strange objects grow back twice as fast."; after a fire that went out, "...so this one will only be long and dark."), and on the night, first, what it does and how the fire stands ("The lodge's fire needs feeding tonight: 18 minutes left. If it lasts until dawn, next week's Long Night keeps its bonus." or "The lodge's fire went out tonight: next week's Long Night will only be long and dark."). Walt, by that fire, asks for fuel before anything else ("Long Night tonight. Nobody keeps this fire alone tonight, not me either: it wants resin and cloth from whoever's about, till dawn. There's about 18 minutes in it."), or says it went out.

**What the woods are like today.** The map never changes, only what happens on it, so the tenth trip is not the same as the first. Every day, from dawn (when the night ends), the Near Woods draws one condition, and half the days a second one, from a list in `content/items.json`; and one **weekly** condition everyone shares comes round in a fixed order, so nothing is missed for good. Weeks turn on Monday at 00:00 UTC, which is always a dawn. All of it follows the wall clock, like the weather, so everyone has the same day and a restart changes nothing. Conditions are made of four things only: **fog** (you see a few tiles past yourself), **finds** that grow only while the condition is on, the **watchers** waking only farther in (or closer, or not at all), and a shelter **fire going out** overnight (never the old cabin's).
**Built:** twelve daily conditions: thick fog (you see about 5 tiles), a NAPO cache the creek washed out of its bank (3 scrap and 3 wire by the pond, about 50 steps out, that do not grow back that day: first come, first served; nothing comes into the Zone from outside any more), the watchers moved north (they wake only 80 steps or more from home) or came closer (from 40), a shelter fire went out, glowcaps everywhere, the firs weeping resin, the cabin at the end opened (cloth), coffee in the shelters, flares in the old car, shards near the road (by the poles, 20 to 60 steps out), scrap by the old car. Two weekly ones: copper week (wire by every pole) and quiet woods (the watchers sleep). At dawn a banner and a soft chime say what today brings, Mira starts with the news ("Word from the woods today: thick fog, and a NAPO cache. This week: copper week."), and the notice board lists today's with what each means, this week's and next week's. The creek rising (the ford closed) waits for tiles that can close for a day.

## Hazards, anomalies and resistances

Like in Pacific Drive, the world itself wears you down, on top of the steady drain of being out there. Five elements, each with its own resistance:

| Element | Comes from (examples) |
|---|---|
| **Heat** | the burnt forest, summer afternoons, fire anomalies |
| **Cold** | night, winter, the snowy ridge, being wet in the wind |
| **Wind** | storms, open ridges and clearings |
| **Electricity** | electrical storms, flashes, broken power lines |
| **Radiation** | glowing anomalies, shards, the deepest places |

- **Anomalies are events**, shared by everyone on the map and announced before they hit so you can react: a **storm** rolls in over a region (wind, electricity, less to see); a **flash** marks a patch of ground that glows and then discharges; a **surge** that starts at the deepest place and rolls toward home. Being caught costs energy, less with the right resistance. Some anomalies leave rare finds behind.
- **Surges are built** (before the elements): each region can have a surge clock, fixed to the wall clock so everyone learns it ("the woods surge at a quarter to"). The Near Woods: every 40 minutes, 6 restless, then a surge of 2.5 minutes. Restless, rare finds show up deep in (shards, strange objects), and they are gone when the calm comes back: the best loot sits right before the danger. The surge's front starts at the deepest tile and reaches the way home in 90 seconds; wherever it has passed, away from a street light, energy drains 3 times as fast. Every phase is announced with a banner, and a clock shows in the status panel.
- **Storms and flashes are built.** Each region can have a storm clock, fixed to the wall clock like its surges. The Near Woods: every 40 minutes, halfway between two surges, a storm is announced a minute before and blows for 3 minutes. Out in it energy drains 1.5 times as fast (wind and electricity each cut half of the extra), it soaks you like rain, and being wet in its wind chills you half as much again (cold). The sky darkens, the fog closes in, lightning blinks. While it blows, shards turn up by the old poles. A flash starts every minute and a quarter near someone 30 steps or more out, often right under them: the ground glows for 8 seconds, faster and brighter, then discharges for 4 (a spark is electric, a fire flash hot); standing in it drains 6 times as fast. The status panel says what drains you, element by element.
- A shelter protects from everything outside. Resistance never makes you immune: it cuts the loss (a percentage per element).

## Equipment and stats

Six slots: **cap, shirt, gloves, pants, shoes, bag**. Better equipment means better resistances, so you can stay out longer and go where the weather or the anomalies would stop you.

- Each piece gives resistances to some elements (a raincoat: wind and cold; rubber gloves and boots: electricity; a lead-lined cap: radiation). Some also give a little extra energy.
- The **bag** decides how many slots you carry (a tote 6, a backpack 8, a hiking pack 12, an expedition pack 16).
- Gear comes in tiers (worn, sturdy, rugged, expedition, anomalous). It comes from **crafting at the workbench** (at home, beside the chest) with what you bring home, from rare finds deep in, and from trading with friends.
- Gear goes on and comes off at the chest at home, and anywhere from and into the bag. A piece can travel in the bag: it takes a slot of its own, weighs what it weighs, and keeps its condition, quirk and level wherever it goes (the chest, the bag, a pile, someone else's half of one, later a trade). The bag you wear changes only at home, at the chest, where what you carry is checked against it. Choosing what to wear and what to carry is part of planning a trip: a spare costs a slot and weight, and pays for them when a piece wears out far from home. What you wear is kept when you collapse; a piece in the bag falls into the pile like anything you carry, and a watcher may take one like anything else. Your character wears what you equip, so others see your gear, unless you wear an outfit, which changes only how you look ([Look and feel](#look-and-feel)).
  **Built** ([gear-on-the-road](../roadmap/gear-on-the-road.md)): the bag shows a Wearing row under what you carry, your six pieces with how much of each is left, anywhere you are. A tap on a worn piece shows its card, whose button takes it off into the bag, "Take off (it goes into your bag)", greyed out with a word when the bag has no slot free; the bag you wear says it changes only at home. A tap on a carried piece shows its card, whose button puts it on, "Wear (your worn cap goes into your bag)", or "Wear" when nothing is worn there: what it replaces takes its slot, so there is always room. Throw away sits beside it, and asks first with its name. A carried bag cannot be put on from the bag; it goes into the chest and is worn from the stash. At the chest a piece in the stash has Take it out beside Wear, and comes out as it is, one piece to a slot. Putting on and taking off ask nothing: nothing is used up.
- **A tap looks, a second step acts** ([gear-details](../roadmap/gear-details.md)): in the chest and the bag, a tap on a piece shows what it is (its tier, what it resists, the energy it adds, how worn it is, its quirk and level); a double tap, or the button in what the tap showed, puts it on or takes it off. The bag already works this way for everything else. **Built:** in the chest and at the workbench a tap on anything opens its card, and only its one button, A, or a second tap on the same thing within about a third of a second does something. A piece's card shows its drawing in its color, its name with its level once it has one ("Raincoat +3"), its tier and the slot it goes in, what it resists and the energy it adds as worn as it is (with what it gives when mended), how worn it is as a bar and in words ("Like new", "Worn: 40% left", "Worn out: it protects nothing until it is mended"), and its quirk with what it does. Its button wears it, saying what goes into the stash in its place, or takes it off; the bag is never taken off (its card says how to change it), a bag too small for what you carry cannot be worn, and a worn-out piece says it can be mended at the workbench beside the chest. At the workbench a row opens the card of what it makes, mends or upgrades, with what that takes against what the stash holds, and Make, Mend or Upgrade, which ask first in the text box ([ask-first](../roadmap/ask-first.md)); greyed out, they shake and the text box says what the stash lacks. Everything else in the chest opens its card too, with Put away or Take out (a NAPO lockbox, with Open: it never leaves the chest). B closes the card first, then the panel. The card opens under the list in portrait and beside it in landscape, inside the panel. The bag works the same way, anywhere: a tap on anything in it, or on a piece in its Wearing row, opens its card, whose button (and A, and a double tap) does its one thing (Drink, Light it, Wear, Take off...), with Throw away beside it for what you carry; using and throwing away ask first, with the card still open behind the question.
- **Upgrades, +1 to +9** (**built**: [gear-upgrades](../roadmap/gear-upgrades.md)): at the workbench, a piece you wear or keep in the stash goes up one level at a time, paid from the stash with materials that grow rarer toward the top. +1 takes 2 scrap and 2 cloth; +2, 3 scrap, 2 cloth and a wire; +3, 4 scrap, 3 cloth and 2 wire; +4, 4 scrap, 2 wire and a shard; +5, 5 scrap, 3 wire and 2 shards; +6, 6 scrap, 4 wire and 3 shards; +7, 4 shards and a strange object; +8, 5 shards and 2 strange objects; +9, 6 shards and 3 strange objects (`upgrades` in `content/items.json`). Up to +6 an upgrade always works; +7, +8 and +9 work 70%, 50% and 30% of the time, and a failure costs only the materials: the piece keeps its level, never breaks and never goes down. Each level makes a piece resist 5% more of what it resists (a raincoat's 35% against wind is 37% at +1 and 51% at +9) and wear 5% more slowly (a sturdy piece lasts 90 minutes out there, about 130 at +9); resistances still stop at 75% in all, so no upgrade makes anyone immune. Worn clothes (they resist nothing) and bags (their size is the point) are not upgraded; every other tier is, anomalous gear too. Mending costs the same at any level. The level shows after the name everywhere a piece is named ("Raincoat +3"), and in gold on its slot; it stays with the piece in the chest, on you, in the bag and in a pile. The workbench lists each piece that can go higher under Upgrade, between Mend and Make, with its next level, what that takes against the stash and how often it works; a tap shows its card with what the next level changes ("Wind 35% → 37%", "Lasts 90 min → 95 min"), and its Upgrade button asks first ("Upgrade your raincoat to +7? It uses 4 shards and a strange object. It works 7 times in 10."). The text box then says what happened: "The raincoat is +7 now.", or "It did not take. The raincoat stays +6, and the materials are gone."
- **Built:** everyone starts in worn clothes (they resist nothing) and a backpack (8 slots). At the chest at home gear goes on from the stash and comes off into it: a tap on a piece you wear, or on gear in the stash, shows it, and its button (or a double tap) takes it off into the stash or puts it on (what it replaces goes into the stash); the bag can be changed there, never taken off, and a smaller one has to hold what you carry. The **workbench at home** stands beside the chest by the fire, so making, mending and choosing gear happen in one room, from one stash; it makes gear from what your stash holds, into your stash (recipes are data: `recipes` in `content/items.json`). The lodge, where it used to stand, stacks its firewood there now. Sturdy gear takes cloth, resin, scrap and wire; rugged and expedition gear takes shards too; anomalous gear only comes out of strange objects. Cloth, which most recipes and every mend below anomalous need, grows by the wrecks, barrels and cabins of the Near Woods (four at a time, back in about 5 to 10 minutes), two to a room where the empty house's, the shelters' and NAPO's rooms hold it, and one in each of the four houses in town whose people left (back in 1.5 to 3 hours): about 44 an hour for everyone, a raincoat in two good trips. Bags: a backpack 8, a hiking pack 12, an expedition pack 16. Some pieces add energy to the bar.
- **What resistances do so far** (the five elements are in Hazards below): **cold** softens the extra drain of rain, night and aurora and of being wet, and winter's cold (Seasons); **wind** slows how fast rain soaks you (a raincoat); **electricity** and **radiation** each cut half of a surge's extra drain, so full protection takes both; **wind** and **electricity** each cut half of a storm's extra drain; **heat** cuts a fire flash's, **electricity** a spark's (welding gloves are the first heat gear). Resistances add up over what you wear, and the effects working on you (a hand warmer, a rad tablet: Items), and stop at 75%.
- **Gear wears out, and rare gear has quirks (built).** Each piece has a condition. Out in the wilds, what you wear wears down: sturdy gear in 90 minutes out there, rugged in 2 hours, expedition in 2.5, anomalous in 3 (worn clothes and bags never do). Below a quarter left it protects less and less, and gives less energy; worn out, nothing, until it is mended at the workbench (sturdy: 2 cloth and 1 scrap; rugged: 3 and 2; expedition: 4 and 3; anomalous: 2 scrap and a shard), which makes it whole again. A piece keeps its condition in the stash and in the bag; the chest and the bag show each piece on its own, and you pick which one to put on, so a worn piece never comes back new. Gear a strange object turns into is a piece the moment it lands in your bag, and can be worn at once. Anomalous pieces have a quirk, rolled when a piece comes to be (in your stash, on you, or in your bag): **glowing steps** (out there your footprints glow for half a minute, for everyone to follow), **restless light** (street lights flicker as you pass, for everyone to see) and **humming** (a minute before your region grows restless, before anyone is told). The Wearing row (in the bag and at the chest), the bag and the stash show how much is left of each piece, and the Status panel what is wearing down and your quirks.

## Items

Everything is data (`content/items.json`: name, what it is, stack size, stats, where it grows), so the list can grow and be tuned without code. A first set:

- **Resources** (for crafting and XP): glowcaps (they paint marks), fir resin and cloth scraps (they burn), scrap metal (heavy), copper wire, anomaly shards (rare, deep in; the Old Stone wants them).
- **Consumables** (used from the bag): a thermos (+30 energy), a road flare (45 seconds of red light: creatures keep off, a hitchhiker lets go), hand warmers (cold resistance for a while), rad tablets (radiation resistance for a while).
  **Built** ([regional-weather](../roadmap/regional-weather.md)): **hand warmers** give cold resistance +40% for 5 minutes, **rad tablets** radiation resistance +40% for 5 minutes: effects, on top of what you wear and under the same 75% cap, so nothing makes anyone immune. A second of the same while the first still works does not add up: it starts the 5 minutes again (a hand warmer and a rad tablet together both count). Used from the bag like a thermos, they ask first ("Use a hand warmer? Cold resistance +40% for 5 minutes.", or while one still works, "The one before still works for 3 minutes. This one starts the 5 minutes again: it does not add up.") and then say what they did. They run on the wall clock, while you are away too. They are NAPO's first-aid stores: one of each lies in the NAPO Bunker (Ruth's) and in the stores, and rad tablets in the laboratory, each back 20 to 40 minutes after it is taken; and the workbench makes 2 hand warmers from 2 cloth and 1 scrap, and 3 rad tablets from 4 glowcaps and 1 resin (NAPO pressed its tablets from glowcaps, its label says, and so can you). Hand warmers stack 4 to a slot, rad tablets 6; each earns 3 XP stashed.
- **Strange objects**, found deep in (more while a region is restless): what they are shows only when you look at them in town, in the light (a house in town counts). Mostly shards, a thermos or flares; now and then a charm, or a piece of anomalous gear (a shard-lined cap), which is a piece in your bag at once and can be worn there and then.
- **Charms** work while they are in your bag, one of each kind: a warm pebble (rain soaks you 40% slower), a hollow feather (your bag feels 25% lighter), a humming bead (hitchhikers find you 60% less often).
- **Live finds** turn up only while a region is restless, and fade on the way home. **Built:** the three shards that grow deep in the Near Woods while it is restless are **live shards**. One is worth 40 XP stashed within 4 minutes of picking it up, then 5 XP less a minute until, at 10 minutes, it is a plain anomaly shard (12 XP). The clock runs on the wall clock, so it fades while you are offline too. While you carry one, a pale column of light stands over you that everyone on the map sees from beyond the fog, and watchers come for you from farther. Put down (dropped, lost to a watcher, or in the pile when you collapse) it goes dim for good; the Old Stone takes it like any shard. The bag shows a ring that empties and "Worth 40 XP for 3:12 more", then "Fading: 30 XP now"; picking one up says "It is still live". How long it stays fresh is data (`live` in `content/items.json`), to tune after play-testing.
- **A NAPO lockbox** ([daily-parcels](../roadmap/daily-parcels.md)), sealed since the evacuation, comes in Sunday's parcel to whoever played all week, and is opened at the chest: shards, a strange object, a charm, or a good stack of cloth and wire. **Built:** it stays in the chest (it is never taken out, carried or lost in a pile). A tap shows its card, with what it may hold; its button asks first ("Open the NAPO lockbox? It has been sealed since the evacuation."), and the text box says what was inside: 3 shards (3 times in 10), a strange object (2 in 10), a charm, any of them alike (2 in 10), or 6 cloth and 4 wire (3 in 10), as data (`holds` in `content/items.json`). What it held goes into the stash and earns no XP, like the parcel it came in; a strange object from it, taken out and looked at, turns into something that earns nothing either (the stash rule, in The loop).
- Every item has a **weight** (it shows in the bag with what burns and what a charm does).
- **Equipment**: a few pieces per slot and tier, starting with worn clothes everyone has.
- **Tools: made at the workbench or found, then in the bag's header for good.** Each is a button there (every paper map is the one map button), in the order you got them. A tool is never used up, takes no slot, weighs nothing, never goes into a pile, the stash or a trade, and nothing takes it away. Making one asks first like anything made ("Make a radio? It uses 2 wire and 1 scrap."), and the box then says where it went: "You make a radio. It is yours for good: its button is in your bag." Picked up, one floats over your head like any find ("+ Radio"). You own each one once: at the workbench its card says "You have it", its button greyed (pressed, the box says why), and a find of one you have stays where it lies, for someone else. Tapping one says what it is; what each does comes with it: the radio ([crackle-radio](../roadmap/crackle-radio.md)), the bolt cutters and waders ([locked-places](../roadmap/locked-places.md)), the maps found farther out ([far-woods](../roadmap/far-woods.md), [inked-maps](../roadmap/inked-maps.md)). **Built:** each player's tools are saved with them; everyone starts with the three paper maps.
- **The field radio** ([crackle-radio](../roadmap/crackle-radio.md)): searching deep should be a skill, not a stroll, with nothing on the screen to point the way (Pacific Drive's scanners). **Built:** an old NAPO field radio, rewired at the workbench at home from 2 copper wire and 1 scrap: it asks first like anything made ("Make a radio? It uses 2 wire and 1 scrap.") and goes to your tools for good, and the box says what it does ("Out in the wilds it crackles louder the nearer you are to something strange. Its button turns it off and on."). Its button in the bag's header, a boxy radio with an aerial, turns it on and off: a small lamp in its corner glows green while it is on, and this browser keeps the choice, like the sound setting (on, to start, so a radio just made is heard at once). While on, out in the wilds it crackles louder the closer you are to a shard, a live shard, a strange object or, on aurora nights, copper wire lying out there: loud within 6 tiles, faint within 15, nothing past that, the nearest one deciding, and the clicks come thicker as it heats up. It hears only what your game already knows lies on your map, never shows a direction or a place and never pans: you walk and listen, hotter and colder. Everywhere, town and rooms too, it gives the hum, low and slow, the Old Stone's; on each of the Tower's pulses (your region growing restless) one burst of static; in a storm it goes silent. What it listens for and how far is data (`senses` on the item in `content/items.json`).

## Status

A status tab (in the menu, and a tap on the status panel top left opens it) shows your level and XP, energy (maximum, now, and what is draining it at the moment, element by element), your five resistances with where they come from (each piece of gear), and any effects running (a hand warmer, a storm you are in). **Built:** each effect working on you has a row of its own right under Resists, with what it gives and its time left, counted down ("Hand warmer: Cold +40% for 3:25 more"), and Resists counts it in.

Built so far: energy, how wet you are, your load, what clings to you, your charms, the surge, the Old Stone, and your **feats**. Feats are small perks for good, earned by what you do out there, not bought, and each climbs five ranks as what counts toward it adds up: the first comes in the first hours, the last after months of playing, never from grinding (like The Long Dark's skills).

**Built:** eight feats, five ranks each, every rank a little stronger than the one before:

- **Rain walker** (steps out in the rain): rain soaks you 20, 30, 40, 45 and 50% slower, at 1,500, 5,000, 15,000, 40,000 and 100,000 steps.
- **Night owl** (steps out in the dark): hitchhikers find you 50, 60, 70, 75 and 80% less often, at 1,000, 3,500, 10,000, 25,000 and 60,000 steps.
- **Pack mule** (steps out with a heavy bag): what you carry feels 15, 20, 25, 28 and 30% lighter, at 800, 2,500, 8,000, 20,000 and 50,000 steps.
- **Fire keeper** (fires fed): fires warm you 15, 20, 25, 28 and 30% faster, at 20, 60, 200, 500 and 1,200 fires.
- **Mender** (pieces mended at the workbench): gear wears 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25% slower out there, at 5, 15, 40, 100 and 250 pieces.
- **Forager** (finds picked up out in the wilds, the shelters out there included; not piles, and not in town): a find comes up double 5, 8, 11, 13 and 15% of the time, at 200, 700, 2,000, 5,000 and 12,000 finds. Never a live find, and the second one only if the bag has room for it.
- **Pathfinder** (steps out in the wilds 85 or more walking steps from home): that far out, and only there, the drain is 3, 6, 9, 12 and 15% gentler, at 500, 1,500, 5,000, 12,000 and 30,000 steps.
- **Good neighbor** (thanks received, Cooperation): your arrows last 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7 days instead of one, at 25, 75, 200, 500 and 1,200 thanks. The one feat others earn for you: whoever is thanked most leaves the arrows that last longest, and the question before painting one says how long it will show.

Rank 1 of the first four is what those feats were before they had ranks, at the same counts, and what was counted before carries over: whoever had counted more already is at that rank when they come back, without a banner. A new rank is announced once, with a banner ("Rain walker, rank 2" and "Rain soaks you 30% slower."), as soon as no panel, text box or other banner is in the way. The status panel shows each feat with its rank as five pips, what the rank does (before rank 1, what rank 1 will do), and a bar with how far the next rank is ("3,212 of 5,000 steps in the rain to rank 2"); at rank 5, "Top rank".

Your level shows next to your name, and the Status panel starts with your level and the XP to the next one, and at level 20 with your merits ("Merits: 3 to spend, 1,240 XP to the next"); a guest's starts with what playing as a guest means, and the Sign in button ([Play first, sign in to keep it](#together-chat-and-friends)).

## Together: chat and friends

- **World chat** for everyone online, and **local chat** for whoever is near you: it shows as a speech bubble over your head (FireRed style) and in the chat log. Reached from the chat button next to the menu (Enter on a keyboard), which shows a dot when there is something new.
  **Built:** the chat button beside the menu opens it (Enter opens it with its line ready to type in), with a Near you tab (your map, 12 tiles around you; a bubble over the speaker's head for 6 seconds) and an Everyone tab. Only signed-in players talk: a guest hears what is said, and where the line to type in would be, finds one card that says signing in lets them chat, with the Sign in button. 120 characters a message, 5 every 10 seconds. Words on the list (`content/words.json`) are masked with asterisks, however they are spelled. Nothing said is kept or logged; your game keeps the last 100 lines until you close it. Tapping a name opens that player's card, to befriend, block (you no longer hear them) or report them (with the line they wrote).
- **Friends**: send a request (tap a player, or by name), accept or decline; the friends list shows who is online and where they are. **Private messages** between friends, kept until read.
  **Built:** Friends in the menu (a dot there when someone asked you or wrote you): ask by name, answer requests, see friends online and on which map, open someone's card to write to them, unfriend, block or report them. Tapping a player's name tag opens their card. Friends need sign-in on both sides: a guest opening Friends (or someone's card) finds one card that says so, with the Sign in button, and a guest's card says they play as a guest instead of offering "Ask to be friends"; a guest can still be blocked and reported, and cannot report until they sign in, since a report needs someone who answers for it. Messages are deleted from the server once read, so a conversation lasts only as long as the session. Blocking ends a friendship and keeps them from asking or writing again; whoever is blocked hears only that you take no requests. Reports are kept for the maintainers with what was written, as the reporter quoted it. A setting turns friend requests off. Limits: 200 characters a message, 20 a minute, 50 unread from one friend, 20 requests waiting. The word filter comes with chat.
- **Calls without words** ([calls](../roadmap/calls.md)): nobody types while walking on a phone, so strangers who meet out there need another way to work together. Hold B and your character sings a short note in a voice of its own, heard 25 tiles around from the side it comes from, fainter the farther away: sound alone, never a marker (Journey's chirp).
  **Built:** B held 300 ms or more, with no panel, card, text box or question open, opens a fan of three calls above B, up and to the left, clear of A: **here I am** (one note), **come here** (a note held and rising) and **thank you** (two quick notes). Slide the finger onto one and let go to sing it; let go without sliding, and it is here I am; slide off the fan (toward A, or down) and letting go sings nothing. A tap on B still opens the bag or goes back, as ever. On a keyboard, hold Q, then press 1, 2 or 3 (or let go for here I am). The fan says each call in words under its note the first five times this browser sings one, then the notes alone. Each player's voice is one note of a pentatonic scale, picked from who they are, the same every day, so friends learn each other's voices; the calls are told apart by their shape, never their pitch. Everyone on your map within 25 tiles hears it (you too), panned to the side it comes from and quieter and duller the farther away, and if the caller is on screen a little note in their jacket color rises over their head for a second, drawn in the shape of the call. Nothing shows at the screen's edge, and nothing says where a call came from but your ears. Anyone may call, guests too, since there are no words to filter; one call every 2 seconds ("Catch your breath first" otherwise), and someone who blocks you does not hear your calls, as they do not hear you in chat. The sound setting's volume and mute apply.
- **Exchange, face to face only**: two friends on the same map, at most **10 tiles** apart, open a trade: each puts in items or gear, both confirm the final offer, and the server swaps them in one step (nothing can go missing halfway). Walking farther apart cancels it.
- **Settings** (in the menu): anyone can turn off friend requests and trade requests.
- Safety from day one: limits on length and speed, a word filter, block and report.
- **Play first, sign in to keep it** ([play-first](../roadmap/play-first.md)): a first visit should end in the woods, not at an email form. A first-time visitor plays at once, as a guest whose character lives in that browser. Chat, friends, trading, the daily parcels and outfits need sign-in, since chat, friends and items are worth keeping safe; the moment it is worth it to them, signing in keeps everything.
  **Built:** the first card is the game's name, its one line, a name and a big **Play now**; "I have played before: sign in" is the quiet link under it. The name makes a **guest**: a character like the ones made before sign-in, whose login token lives in that browser (the server keeps only a scrambled copy) and nothing says who plays it. That browser comes straight back in as its guest, no card. Top left, under the name, the status panel says **Guest** with a **Sign in** button, and the Status tab (a tap on the panel opens it) says it plainly: a guest's progress lives in this browser and is lost with its data, a guest who stays away for 30 days is deleted (with their pile and marks; never before the rule has been in the game for 30 days, so characters from before sign-in, which count as guests, get a month's notice), and signing in keeps everything. Sign in, from there or from the menu, and the character becomes the account's (the claim below): "Signed in. Your character is yours to keep." An account keeps one character: if it has one already, it stays the account's, and the sign-in card says so before anything changes ("This account already has a character, Aldo. Play as Aldo? Your guest character stays in this browser."), with the way back to the guest; signing out brings the guest back. What a guest cannot use yet is never greyed out without a word: the chat and the friends panel each show one card that says what signing in opens, with the button. Signing in is **an email and a one-time code, or a Google or Apple account (below), through Supabase Auth**. The game server checks the Supabase login and keeps its own players and game data in its own database; a character made without sign-in (a guest, or one from before sign-in), which lives only in the browser that made it, is claimed by signing in on that browser, and from then on its token alone plays it no more.
- **Google and Apple** ([sign-in-google-apple](../roadmap/sign-in-google-apple.md)): on a phone, one tap is easier than typing a code from an email, so signing in offers both, through the same Supabase sign-in.
  **Built:** the sign-in card offers **Continue with Google** and **Sign in with Apple** above the email, drawn as each company's guidelines ask, with a quiet "or with your email" between them and the email; only those the server lists, since each has to be set up with Supabase first (until then the card is the email alone). A tap goes to Google's or Apple's own page and comes back into the game signed in, and a guest keeps their character exactly as with the code (or is asked first, when the account has a character of its own). A sign-in that does not finish, cancelled or refused, comes back to the card with one plain line, "Signing in with Google did not finish. Try again, or use your email.", and a guest's way back to playing under it. The same email address is the same account, however you sign in with it; Apple's hidden addresses are new ones.

## Look and feel

- Low-poly 3D with the structure of Pokemon FireRed: a steep, almost top-down camera over a tile grid, ledges and walls of trees, chunky chibi characters, two-tone toon shading with outlines, a text box at the bottom for talking.
- A **dark world** in the mood of **Pacific Drive**: a Pacific Northwest forest of tall firs, fog, rain, night, abandoned cabins, orange sodium street lights, utility poles, glowing anomalies (the Old Stone, wisps), and the old facilities, antennas and warning signs NAPO left behind ([the story](#the-story-napo-and-napoland)).
- **NAPO's things look like NAPO's:** concrete buildings with flat roofs rimmed in NAPO yellow (concrete inside too, not logs), yellow warning signs edged in black, desks whose screens still glow green, and the Tower, a red and white mast with a dish turned toward the woods and a red light that blinks day and night. Each townsperson has colors of their own; NAPO's old lineman still wears his yellow hard hat.
- **Outfits** ([outfits](../roadmap/outfits.md)): how your character looks, whatever gear it wears, with no stats. Everyone gets the same first one with their first sign-in: a NAPO work suit from the station's stores. More come with levels (the lineman's jacket at 5, the survey rain cape at 10, the ranger's coat at 15, the residents' patchwork at 20). Past level 20, merits buy jacket patterns and name tag badges ([past-twenty](../roadmap/past-twenty.md)), and later a shop sells looks only, never power, and never one that can be earned ([looks-shop](../roadmap/looks-shop.md)). The pack you carry still shows over any outfit, and your color (arrows, name) stays yours.
  **Built:** the chest at home has two tabs, the stash (what came in parcels and the first goal at its top, then your bag, what you wear and the stash) and the wardrobe, which opens on its outfits (its patterns and badges are below, past level 20): a tile for no outfit (your gear shows, as before) and one for each outfit, a small figure in its colors: a locked one is dim and says the level that opens it ("Level 10"), the one you wear has a tick. A tap shows its card (its name, its line, and Wear or Take off; greyed out, with the level that opens it, while it is locked), and its button, A or a double tap wears it; B backs out. It asks nothing first, since nothing is used up. Nobody starts wearing one: the suit waits in the wardrobe from the first sign-in, and the welcome parcel's banner says so, so everyone's gear shows until they choose. Which outfits you have is never stored: it follows from signing in and from your level, so nothing is ever lost or has to be handed out. A guest's wardrobe is one card, "Sign in to keep what you wear. Signing in keeps your character.", with the Sign in button, and a guest who signs in has theirs at once. A level that opens an outfit says so in its banner ("New in your wardrobe: the lineman's jacket."; a guest reads what signing in would open), shown once the chest is closed, and puts a dot on the Wardrobe tab until it is looked at. Everyone on the map sees a change at once, and whoever comes later sees it too. In the world each outfit is its own colors and a few low-poly parts: the suit's grey coverall with a belt, NAPO's yellow patch and a grey cap; the lineman's orange jacket with pale bands and Walt's yellow hard hat; the rain cape, a green bell over the arms with a yellow hem, its hood up; the ranger's brown wool coat to the knees with a brass badge and a campaign hat; the patchwork's four cloths, a knitted cap and its bobble. The pack goes on over any of them.
- **Past level 20** ([past-twenty](../roadmap/past-twenty.md)): the players who play most keep the loop's reward. Level 20 stays the top, but every 1,500 XP stashing earns past it (past 10,830) is a merit, and merits buy looks others see, never power: a pattern on your jacket or a badge on your name tag, each bought once and kept. How many merits you earned follows from your XP, so nothing is granted and nothing is lost; only what you spent and bought is kept. Later, glowcap mark shapes too.
  **Built:** six jacket patterns (stripes, checks, a chevron, reflective bands, a NAPO patch, patchwork squares) and six name tag badges (a fir, a flame, a shard, a lamp, a moth, the Old Stone), each for one merit. The wardrobe has three tabs of its own under the chest's: Outfits, Patterns and Badges, each a tile for none and one for each look, in the card model of the outfits: a tap shows its card (its drawing, name and line, where it goes); one not yours yet shows its price, "1 merit", bright while a merit can buy it and dim while none can, and its card's Buy button asks first ("Spend a merit on the chevron pattern? You have 3."), with the card still open behind the question, then the text box says what it did ("The chevron pattern is yours for good. 2 merits left to spend."); greyed out, its card says why ("You have no merit to spend. 1,240 XP to the next."). Yours, its button, A or a double tap wears it, and wearing asks nothing: one pattern and one badge at a time, over an outfit too. Over the patterns and the badges the wardrobe says what merits there are ("Merits: 3 to spend, 1,240 XP to the next."), and below level 20 what is coming ("Past level 20, every 1,500 XP earns a merit."), the looks dim with their price. Each merit earned says so in a banner once the chest is closed ("A merit"), and puts a dot on the Patterns and Badges tabs until they are looked at; level 20's banner says merits come next. Everyone on the map sees a pattern or a badge at once, and whoever comes later sees it too; put on at the chest in your own cabin, where nobody else is, it shows as you walk out. A pattern is drawn on the jacket in a shade of its cloth (darker on a light cloth, lighter on a dark one) or in colors of its own (the pale tape of the bands, NAPO's yellow, the squares' cloths), low on the front, where the camera sees past the big head, and on the sleeves, which show from the side; on the survey rain cape it goes on the cape's bell, which hides the arms. A badge sits before the name on a name tag, as tall as its letters, and before your own name at the top of the screen, where you see what others see. A guest earns merits like anyone and spends them once signed in: the wardrobe's one card says so, with the Sign in button.
- **Grass that looks like grass.** **Built:** on every map the ground's color drifts the way a meadow's does, paler where it is dry, deeper where it is damp, dark and olive under the edge of the trees, a touch different from tile to tile, and always the same in the same place; the old asphalt, the paths and the mud vary too. Low tufts grow on the grass, and the tall grass (Creatures) sways, harder in a storm, and parts around whoever wades into it.
- **The places tell the story** ([richer-places](../roadmap/richer-places.md)). **Built:** what the town, the leavers and NAPO left stands where they left it (see [the story](#the-story-napo-and-napoland) and [World structure](#world-structure)), in the same low-poly toon look: the leavers' houses dark behind curtains in faded cloth, the sawmill long and low under a rusted sawtooth roof with its burner's stack, cars in the paint they had, NAPO's trucks, pump, cages and stakes in NAPO's off-white and yellow, a faint glow in the rocks it caged. None of it moves: it joins the few meshes a map's props are already drawn as ([ARCHITECTURE.md](ARCHITECTURE.md#rendering)).
- Dark interface: dark panels with cream borders, Fredoka and Nunito fonts.
- **Sound** carries the mood and the warnings, so a thumb on the stick does not have to read to stay alive. **Built:** every sound is made in the browser (Web Audio), nothing is downloaded. Rain outdoors (faint through a roof), wind in the wilds and harder in a storm, a fire that crackles loud when fed and quieter as it burns low and as you walk away, footsteps that change on road, grass and ferns, mud, water and plank floors, with a swish through tall grass (only yours), and the power lines humming near the poles on aurora nights. Warnings: a low bell when a region grows restless and a low tone while it stays so, a rumble that rises as the surge front comes toward you and a hard drone once it is on you, a rising tone when a storm is coming, thunder a moment after each lightning blink, a crackle when a flash starts near you and a pop when it bursts, a dragging sound while a watcher moves, gone the moment it freezes, and ferns thrashing while a skulker chases anyone near you, with a sharp cry the moment one goes after you. Calls: each player's own note, sung like a hum through cupped hands, from the side it comes from and duller the farther away. The field radio, while it is on: a crackle of static and clicks that thickens near something strange out in the wilds, the low hum everywhere, a burst of static on each of the Tower's pulses, a click when it is switched. The menu has a Sound row: mute and volume (on at 70% to start), kept by this browser.
- **Any screen shape works:** nothing forces portrait or landscape. The camera shows the same circle of world around you on every screen; controls sit in the thumb corners and are sized from the short side; panels open where they cover the least.

## References

Games we learn from when designing what comes next:

- **Pacific Drive** (Ironwood Studios): the mood (a dark Pacific Northwest forest, rain, abandoned things, glowing anomalies), and the loop of leaving a safe place, pushing your luck out there and coming back changed. Anomalies and storms that wear you down.
- **Pokémon FireRed** (Game Freak): the structure: a tile grid under a steep camera, towns and routes as separate maps, every building enterable, a text box for talking, controls that stay simple.
- **The Long Dark** (Hinterland Studio): survival as quiet pressure: cold, wind and exposure, fire as the thing that keeps you going, cabins to scavenge and shelter in, and maps drawn by hand that never show where you are, so you learn the land by its landmarks.
