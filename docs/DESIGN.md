# napoland: game design

The decisions so far, with the reason for each. Change this file when a decision changes.

## The pitch

**Pacific Drive without the car, as an online game for phones.** You live in a cabin in Stonebrook, a small town at the edge of a strange, dark forest. You go out to gather what the forest gives (resources, power-ups), and you have to come back before your energy runs out. The farther you go, the more dangerous it gets and the better the finds. Alone you can explore the near woods; to go really far, you need other people.

## Pillars

1. **No fights, no PvP, no missions.** Tension comes from the world: distance, energy, weather, night, creatures that chase you, anomalies. Other players are only ever help.
2. **Leave home, come back.** Every trip is a decision: one more find, or turn back now. Trips last 5 to 15 minutes, which suits phones.
3. **Farther is harder and richer.** Difficulty and rewards grow with distance from home.
4. **Together you go farther.** Deep areas need several people (see Cooperation).
5. **One fixed world you learn.** The map does not reshuffle, so players can share guides, routes and strategies. It grows over time as new regions are added farther out.

## The story: NAPO and Napoland

**N.A.P.O., the National Anomalous Phenomena Observatory**, was originally an organization, or a research program, set up to study strange environmental phenomena. Something went wrong. The territory around it became unstable, dangerous, and in the end abandoned or quarantined. People started calling the whole affected region **Napoland**. Stonebrook named it first; NAPO's orders took the name up later, as the Napoland Zone. The game is set there, and the players are Napoland residents.

What happened, as far as anyone in Stonebrook can tell:

1. **The Old Stone.** Stonebrook was a logging town, named for the standing stone by its brook. The stone hummed on cold nights, and nobody minded.
2. **The Observatory.** Then the hum got into the radios and compasses started turning toward the woods, and NAPO came to study it. It bought the land down the south road, on the far side of town from the woods, and built a research station there, a tower to listen with (its dish turned north, to the woods), a bunker, and a checkpoint at the end of its grounds. Half the town worked for it: drivers, cooks, a lineman, a nurse. NAPO found that the hum was not only in the Old Stone: the rocks deep in the woods hummed too, the same hum, as if the stones were answering each other. It set a listening post by them, and carried some of them south to its field site to listen closer.
3. **The answer.** So NAPO answered. One night the Tower sent the hum back into the woods, and the woods replied: light came up between the trees, the first surge rolled out from deep in toward the town, and the Old Stone cracked. It has shed shards ever since, and the stones that hum back pull them in: shards turn up deep in the woods and at NAPO's field site, most of all while the woods are restless, and by the old poles in a storm, as if the hum ran down the wires.
4. **Napoland.** The Tower still pulses every 40 minutes, and the woods answer on the same clock: each surge starts at the deepest place and rolls toward town, and dies at the town's edge, so the south road beyond it stays calm. Every third night the sky goes green. Things turn up that were not there the day before. Tall pale figures stand in the trees and move only while nobody looks at them (NAPO's notes call them observers; in Stonebrook they are watchers).
5. **The quarantine.** NAPO closed the south road at its checkpoint, drew a line on its maps around everything north of it, the Napoland Zone, and told everyone to leave. Most of Stonebrook drove down the south road; NAPO said two weeks. Its crews kept coming in for a while, in white suits, and then they stopped. The station went quiet, nobody has kept the checkpoint for years, and nobody knows whether NAPO still exists.
6. **The residents.** Twenty-three people stayed, or came back (Stonebrook's sign still says "Pop. 23"; nobody has changed it since): the Napoland residents, and every player is one of them. They keep the fires going, bring home what glows and what NAPO left, and feed the shards back to the Old Stone, because when it has enough it wakes and the surges grow gentler, as if it were taking back what the answer broke off it.

- **Why:** it gives a reason for everything the world already shows (surges on a fixed clock, aurora nights, shards, strange objects, watchers, a town most people left), it gives the game its name, and what NAPO left behind makes landmarks in a world you learn by walking it.
- **Where things are**, one logic for the whole map: the woods get deeper to the north, toward the stones that hum back. The Near Woods come first, and every deeper region opens north beyond them ([deeper-regions](../roadmap/deeper-regions.md)). The South Road is the other way: calm, and it ends at NAPO's checkpoint on the quarantine line, the edge of the Napoland Zone (what lies past it is outside the Zone, and still open). The Old Stone is the other end of the hum, not a door. For now every area has its paper map, and the map you open is the one of the area you are in; farther out there will be areas without one, whose maps have to be found.
- **Who tells it**, each in their own words: **Mira** by the notice board (the woods, and how to come back from them); **Walt Pruitt** by the fire in Stonebrook Lodge, who kept the north line for the power company and then NAPO's (he strung its wire from the station to the Tower), went with the others when NAPO said two weeks, came back for his truck and stayed, and remembers the night of the answer; **Ruth** in the NAPO Bunker, NAPO's nurse, who stayed with the ones who would not leave and keeps the bunker's fire going; **Vera** in the NAPO Laboratory, the last of NAPO's researchers, who never left and is still listening. NAPO's own words are in its logs, radios, panels and warning signs, which you read like signs.
- **What NAPO left behind** can appear throughout the map: old NAPO facilities, signs, antennas, laboratories, shelters, warning signs and abandoned equipment. **Built:** the South Road (see [World structure](#world-structure)): the research station (its laboratory, dormitory and stores), the NAPO Tower and its shed, the NAPO Bunker, the field site and the checkpoint on the quarantine line, with NAPO's power line, warning signs, logs and crates of equipment. In the Near Woods, NAPO's Zone warning where the road comes in and its listening post by the ring of stones; in Stonebrook, its evacuation notice by the south road. The lamp in the Near Woods that no wires run to may be NAPO's too. Still to come: more of it in the areas there are ([richer-places](../roadmap/richer-places.md)), and farther out ([napo-in-the-world](../roadmap/napo-in-the-world.md)).
- **Names in the world** come from it, for example: NAPO Research Station, NAPO Tower, NAPO Laboratory, NAPO Equipment, NAPO Bunker, Napoland Zone, Napoland Residents; the answer (the night it went wrong), observers (NAPO's word for the watchers), the quarantine line (where the checkpoint closed the road), the leavers' camp (where the people who left spent their last night).
- **Told by the world, never by a mission** (pillar 1): by what stands out there, what signs and NAPO's logs say, what people tell you and what items are.
- **Figures you see:** the figures of people (the echo of someone's last steps) are their size and see-through, and walk while you watch; the observers are tall, solid and pale-faced, and move only while nobody watches. Nothing else in the world looks like either, so nobody takes one for the other.
- **Chapters: the story you go through.** Each player also goes through the story, chapter by chapter (`content/story.json`). Everyone starts in the first (Home), and every chapter after it is reached by one thing the game already asks of you: bring something home, walk into a place, feed a fire out in the wilds, give the Old Stone a shard, pick up a find, talk to someone or read one of NAPO's desks. So the first chapters teach the game (the stash and XP, fires and energy, fires that burn down unless someone feeds them), and the later ones take you down the South Road to what NAPO did. So far: Home; What glows; The old cabin; Whoever comes next; The lineman; The bunker; What to do during an event; The laboratory; The answer; What the answer broke; Do not switch off.
  - **Not a mission** (pillar 1 holds): nothing is asked, timed, rewarded or failed, there is no task list and no marker on the world, and ignoring the story costs nothing. People hint at what comes next in their own words: before what they always say, or after it when talking to them is what moves the story on. What they hint changes as your story moves on.
  - **The journal**, in the menu, keeps the chapters you reached, the latest first. A banner says when a new one is in it (once nobody is talking to you and no panel covers it), and a dot on the menu stays until you open the journal. It is the one book the game keeps for you: what you find out besides the story (field notes, the notes people left) goes into it too, never into a second book.
  - **What people say** is put together in one place, in one order: the hint of the chapter you are in comes first (or last, when talking to them is what moves the story on), then what they always say. Anything added later (what they say about what you did, or about the day) joins that same order.
  - **Never finished:** no percentage and no count of chapters. The latest chapter written is where the story rests until the next one is. New chapters are added at the end as the world grows (with new regions, say), and whoever reached the end reads on from there.
  - **Writing one:** add it at the end of `content/story.json` and bump its version; never remove, rename or reorder a chapter, since each player's place in the story is the id of the latest chapter they reached. `npm run validate` checks that the people, desks, maps and items a chapter names exist. What a chapter says is part of the story: it follows this section, and a new fact goes here first.
- **Still open:** what the stones are and what the hum says; what the observers are; why nobody switches the Tower off; whether NAPO still exists; where the south road goes past the checkpoint; who keeps the old cabin's fire going. A new fact about the story is a design decision: write it here first, so signs, people and items never contradict each other.

## The loop

1. Wake up at home (your cabin). Equip gear at the chest by the fire, and make or mend it at the workbench beside the chest. Choosing what to wear and what to carry is part of planning a trip: out there you can check what you wear and put on a piece you carry, but a carried piece takes a slot and weighs what it weighs (planned: [gear-on-the-road](../roadmap/gear-on-the-road.md); today gear goes on and comes off only at home).
2. Walk out through the town into the wilds. Energy drains in the wilds, faster the farther you are, at night and in the rain; in town and inside buildings it holds. The only way to get it back is **a fireplace**. Fires in town (your home's, the lodge's) are tended and never go out, and so is the fire of the shelter nearest to town in each region next to it (the Near Woods' old cabin, the South Road's NAPO Bunker), so a new player always has one safe fire whichever way they go. **The other fires out in the wilds burn down**: a well-fed one gives energy back at the full rate, a low one (under 3 minutes of fuel) only glows and gives 40%, a dead one gives nothing. Anyone can feed one (A, facing it; it asks how many) with what burns: resin (5 minutes), cloth (1.5); a fire holds 30 at most. So players keep the shelters going for each other without ever meeting, and whoever arrives cold at a dead fire had better carry resin. Tuned so that standing at the edge of the Near Woods in the rain, dry and light, empties a full bar in about 5.5 minutes, and its deepest corner in under 2.
   - **What else wears you down out there** (each a factor on the drain): a **heavy bag** (items weigh something; at 10 kg or more the drain is 40% faster, so every find is a choice), being **wet** (rain soaks you through in 2.5 minutes, anywhere outdoors; soaked drains 50% faster; a burning fire dries you in 25 seconds, a roof slowly), a **surge** (below), and a **hitchhiker**: at night, 25 steps or more from home and away from light, something may cling to your back (50% faster) until you reach a street light, a burning fire, a roof or light a flare.
3. Gather resources and power-ups. What you pick up goes in your bag (limited slots). Finds are shared: when someone takes one, a new one of the same kind grows later somewhere else in the same kind of place (deep finds stay deep).
4. Come home and stash what you carry. Stashing earns XP; levels and home upgrades let you go farther next time.
   - **Built:** the stash is the chest by the fire at home (A, facing it); each player sees only their own things in it. Everything put in earns its item's XP (`xp` in `content/items.json`: a glowcap 1, resin 2, scrap 3, a shard 12, a strange object 15, a charm 25...), once: what you take out and bring back earns nothing again, and what you use up after taking it out (a thermos drunk, resin burned) no longer counts against new finds, so XP cannot be farmed. Reaching level L takes 30 × (L − 1)² XP in all (level 2 at 30, 5 at 480, 10 at 2,430, 20, the top, at 10,830), and every level makes the energy bar 5 bigger (100 at level 1, 195 at 20). Home upgrades wait for your own cabin.
5. **If your energy runs out** you wake up at home. What you carried falls out where you collapsed, as a small pile on the ground (the bag itself is equipment and stays with you, like all equipment). If you go back for it, you get everything. If someone else finds it first, they get a random half and the rest is lost. The pile disappears one hour after you collapsed, and a new collapse replaces your old pile: each player has at most one.

**Coming back** (planned: [daily-parcels](../roadmap/daily-parcels.md)). The residents look after whoever comes back. The first time you sign in, a welcome parcel waits in your chest; after that, the first time you play each calendar day (UTC), signed in, the day's parcel goes into your chest, never into the bag. The parcels follow a calendar of seven days, Monday to Sunday (the week of the weekly conditions), the same for everyone and pinned on the notice board; play all seven and Sunday's parcel also holds a NAPO lockbox. Parcels earn no stash XP (they were not brought home), and missing a day costs only that week's lockbox: nothing is ever taken away. They come from the town's own stores, shared out, since nothing comes into the Zone from outside any more. It asks nothing of you but coming back, so it is not a mission (pillar 1).

**Light is not energy.** Street lights, and later torches and gadgets, let you see where you walk; some areas will be almost dark without them. They do not refill energy, but they shelter you from a surge and shake off a hitchhiker.

**Every building can be entered**, like in FireRed: walk into the door and you are inside a small room (a map of its own). Shelters and some houses have a fireplace; empty houses are dark.

## World structure

| Layer | Who shares it | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Your cabin | Only you (and guests you invite later) | Upgradeable. Holds your chest (your stash, your gear and the wardrobe) and the workbench. |
| Your street | About 30 neighbors | Streets fill up; new streets open as players join. Friends can live on the same street. |
| Town square (Stonebrook) | Everyone | The notice board, the lodge, the road out. Splits into copies of ~150 players when crowded, keeping friends together. |
| The wilds | Everyone | One fixed map split into regions. A region holds a few hundred players; only an overcrowded region gets extra copies. The game remembers which copy a pile is in. |

Everything in the world is shared (the ground, piles, weather, time of day); only inventory and personal items are private.

Regions are separate maps joined at their edges, like FireRed's towns and routes: walking off the end of a road fades to the next map, which greets you with its name. So far: **Stonebrook** (the town); up its north road, **the Near Woods** (depth 1); and down its south road, **the South Road** (depth 1), NAPO's old grounds. Deeper regions will open to the north, beyond the Near Woods; the South Road ends at the quarantine line.

**The South Road** is the other way out of town, and the calmer one: the surges roll from deep in the woods toward town and die at its edge, so none reach the road; no storms or flashes either, nothing watches from its trees and nothing lies in wait in its ferns, but it runs a long way (the checkpoint is about 90 steps from town), so its danger is the distance. North to south: NAPO's first warning sign; the NAPO Bunker, where Ruth keeps the fire going (the road's safe fire, like the old cabin's in the woods), and west of the road the camp the leavers made, with a fire in the open; the cars people left on the shoulders; the NAPO Research Station in its fenced yard (the NAPO Laboratory, where Vera is, the dormitory and the stores); the NAPO Tower in its fence, with its shed and the panel that works it; a bog to the west (the sinks) and NAPO's field site to the east, where shards and strange objects turn up; and at the end the checkpoint on the quarantine line, where a barrier and a truck close the road. Its finds lean on what people left: scrap and flares by the cars, cloth and flares in the stores, copper wire along NAPO's power line and by the masts. Like every area so far, it has its paper map.

## Movement and controls

- **Tile by tile** in four directions, like Pokemon FireRed, at **one speed** (a steady run). No sprint button.
- **Controls:** a **joystick** bottom left (it picks one of four directions), **A** (pick up, talk, open) and **B** (bag, back) bottom right. No text labels on the buttons.
- Joystick: a quick flick in a new direction **turns in place**; holding walks; the direction you already face walks at once; changing direction while walking does not stop.
- Tapping the ground walks there along the shortest path. Tapping a person or a sign walks up to it and talks.
- Top right there are only the **menu button** and the **chat button** beside it. **No minimap and no position marker, ever:** you learn the world by walking it (and players share what they learn). The one map is an old **paper map drawn by hand**, a tool you own ([Items](#items)): it shows the region's roads, trails, cabins and landmarks but never where you are, so you work that out from what you see around you. **Built:** the bag's header has one map button (M on a keyboard) for every map you own, and it opens the map of the area you are in; indoors, of the area outside. For now every area has one, and everyone starts with all three: Stonebrook (drawn by someone who stayed), the Near Woods, and the South Road (traced by hand from NAPO's own map of its grounds). Like every tool, a map is yours for good: it takes no slot, weighs nothing and never falls into a pile. The map covers the screen: forest shaded, paths and clearings left bare, the tall grass hatched, the road, the water, the power line, NAPO's buildings, masts and fences, the cabins, the way home and the places people call by name (the pond, the ring of stones, the long grass, the sinks, the quarantine line) written in by hand, everything a little off where it really is. Tap the paper to see it full size and pan around; B (or M) puts it away. Farther out there will be areas without a map; there the button says so, until you find one.
- **Ask first, then say what it did** ([ask-first](../roadmap/ask-first.md)): anything that uses up what you carry or keep (burning it in a fire, giving the Old Stone a shard, drinking, lighting a flare, crushing a glowcap, looking closely at a strange object, making, mending or throwing away, and later opening, upgrading, cooking and giving) asks first in the text box, like Pokémon's YES/NO, with YES already chosen and, where more than one can go at once, how many. Then the same box says what it did, from the server's answer. Only a trade asks nothing more: both sides already confirm it.
  **Built:** the question comes in the text box the townspeople speak in, with the name of what you deal with on its tab (Fire, The Old Stone, Workbench, or the item): the question, and YES over NO in a frame of their own, YES chosen, with a small caret. Where more than one can go at once (feeding a fire, giving the Old Stone shards, throwing away a stack) it also shows how many, ×1 to start, with − and + beside it: at most what you carry, and at a fire only as many as fit (it holds 30 minutes; the last one may top it up); the Old Stone takes all you give it. Tap YES or NO, tap − or + (held, it counts faster and faster); a tap anywhere outside the box is NO. On the stick or the keyboard, up and down (W and S, the arrows) choose, left and right (A and D, the arrows) count, A (E or Space) says the chosen answer and B (Q or Escape) is NO. Nobody walks while it asks, and a direction held when it closes walks only once pressed again. The questions name what goes and what it does, with the numbers from the items: "Feed the fire resin?", "Give the Old Stone a shard?", "Drink the thermos? +30 energy." (or that your energy is full), "Light a road flare? It burns 45 seconds.", "Crush a glowcap to paint an arrow where you face?", "Look closely at the strange object? It will be used up.", "Make a raincoat? It uses 8 cloth and 4 resin.", "Mend your raincoat? It uses 2 cloth and 1 scrap.", "Throw away 3 resin? It is gone for good." At a fire you feed what burns longest of what you carry. At the workbench, making and mending ask the same way, whether from a card's button, A or a double tap on a row. When the game already knows it cannot happen, the box says why instead: nothing that burns (and what would), a fire someone keeps going, a fire as full as it gets, no shard for the Old Stone (and how it stands), a strange object anywhere but in town, a glowcap indoors or where an arrow already is, what the stash lacks at the workbench (its card's button is greyed, and shakes), a bag too full for whatever a strange object may turn out to be. Said yes to, the question stays up until the server answers, then the box says what it did: "The fire takes 3 resin. It will burn 18 more minutes.", "The Old Stone takes the shard: 13 of 20." (or that it wakes, or how long it stays awake), the energy a drink gave, how long a flare keeps things off, which way the arrow points, "It turns out to be a hollow feather. While it is in your bag, what you carry feels lighter." (every item a strange object may turn into has that line), what was made or mended, how many were thrown away; or, in the same box, why the server said no. It closes with any press (A, B, a tap), and that press does nothing else, or by itself after about 4 seconds (longer for more to read; a thin line along its bottom runs out meanwhile); walking off closes it too. Nothing floats over your head for these any more; picking things up still does.
- The text box, the bag and the other panels (the chest, the workbench, and the card a tap opens in them) **never cover A and B**, so B can always close them. In portrait they sit above the controls; in landscape they take the joystick's side (it hides while you read). Pushing the joystick or tapping the world closes the bag and the menu. A question or what the box says stands above the panel it came from, and A and B answer it first, before the card and the panel.
- Mobile first. On desktop the mouse acts as a finger, and the keyboard is a shortcut, never a need: **WASD** or the **arrow keys** walk (with the joystick's rules), **E** or **Space** is A, **Q** or **Escape** is B, **M** opens the map of where you are, and **Enter** opens the chat, as in Metin2: Enter on an empty line closes it again, and Enter after typing sends and keeps it open for more. Keys are read by where they sit, so WASD and the Q and E beside it are the same keys on any layout (ZQSD, A and E on a French one); M goes by its letter, wherever a layout puts it (right of L on a French one); with several direction keys held, the last one pressed wins. Behind the sign-in cards, and in any text field, the keys stay the page's.

## Creatures

Creatures do not fight. They chase. You are a little faster than them, so you escape by moving away in time. If one catches you, you lose energy and drop something. They do not follow you into town, under a street light or into tall grass.

The first kind are the **watchers** of the Near Woods (three, in its deeper half): tall figures with pale blank faces that come closer **only while nobody on the map looks their way** (faces them, with the four-way facing everyone already has). Face one and it freezes; a friend facing it holds it for you. Out of the light and out of tall grass, within 9 steps, it walks toward you (slower than you walk); reaching you, it takes 15 energy and one thing you carry, then goes away for a minute or two. They never stand in light, next to a fire, in tall grass or near a flare. On aurora nights they are faster. **Built:** someone carrying a live find glows, and watchers come for them from 12 steps instead of 9.

**Built:** the ones that chase are the **skulkers**: something long and low that lies in the ferns of the Near Woods, three of them, only 50 steps or more from home, and only at night (aurora nights too) or while a storm blows over the region; by day they are gone. Lying in wait, one hears you walking within 6 steps, or sees you standing still within 3, unless you are in a street light, by a burning fire, near a flare or in tall grass. Then it comes, a quarter slower than you walk: the ferns thrash where it runs, a sharp cry sounds the moment it goes after you ("Something is after you. Run"), and a rustling that grows as it nears. Walk away in time and you get out. It gives up after 20 seconds, or when you reach a street light, a burning fire's warmth, tall grass, the way out of the region or the edge of its range (it never goes nearer home than 50 steps), and goes back to its lair; it never steps into light, next to a fire, into tall grass or onto an exit, and there are none in town or inside. Reaching you, it costs 20 energy and one bag slot, which falls out as your pile where you stand (so you can go back for it: it replaces an older pile of yours, as a collapse does), then it is gone for a minute or two. A flare sends it off, like a watcher. Where they live and when is data per region (`skulkers` in the map).

**Tall grass hides you.** **Built:** eight patches of knee-high grass in the Near Woods that you wade into at your usual pace and crouch in, only your head and shoulders showing. Seven lie in its deeper half, beside the trails a chased player runs along: north of the pond where the west trail comes down from the fern hollow (the long grass) and south of it on the back way, on the old cabin's west side, at both feet of the rocks (the deer beds, north), on the rim of the ring of stones' glade and west of the cabin at the end; one lies shallow, in the headlight clearing, where a new player finds out what it is before it matters. Creatures never step into tall grass, a chase ends when you reach it, and nothing notices you while you are in it (a watcher you face still freezes: looking is what holds it). It hides you from creatures only: you still tire and get wet there, a hitchhiker can still cling to you, and a surge, a storm or a flash still finds you. The deep ferns are the other way round: they hide the skulkers. The paper maps show the tall grass as a hatch, with a key in the margin, so a route can run from patch to patch.

## Cooperation (how groups go farther)

Built so far, working even when nobody is online at the same time:

- **Shelter fires** (all but the nearest one) burn down and anyone can feed them: you arrive at a fire someone else kept going.
- **Marks**: crush a glowcap (Use in the bag) to paint a glowing arrow on the ground where you stand, pointing where you face, in your jacket color. Everyone sees it for a day; each player keeps their newest 6. Routes spread without a map.
- **The notice board** in Stonebrook, next to Mira: the weather and when it changes, what the woods are like today and this week (and next week's), each region's surge clock, which shelter fires are low or out, the collapses of the last hour, the Old Stone.
- **The Old Stone** wakes when the whole server has fed it 20 shards (A, facing it). Awake, it halves the extra drain of every surge, and burns a shard every 30 minutes; at none left it sleeps, and the count starts again. It is kept across restarts. It is the other end of the hum, not a way anywhere: new regions open north, beyond the Near Woods (see [the story](#the-story-napo-and-napoland)).
- **Echoes**: a pile keeps the last 16 steps its owner walked out there; near it, a see-through figure walks them again and sinks where they fell. You see where it went wrong.

Ideas to build and test:

- **Shared light:** deep areas drain energy fast unless you stand in someone's lantern light; more lanterns mean a bigger safe area.
- **Group gates:** some passages open only with several people pulling at once.
- **Rescue:** friends can carry you home when you collapse near them.
- **Heavy finds:** the best finds need two people to carry.
- **Camps:** a group can build a shared camp that works as a temporary home out in the woods.
- Solo players must still progress: better gear lets them go a bit deeper, just more slowly.

## Weather, seasons, day and night

Each region has its own climate (rainforest, snowy ridge, marsh, burnt forest...), its own weather cycle and seasons, and there is day and night. Weather changes what you find (glowcaps after rain, shards at night, frozen lakes you can cross in winter) and how dangerous it is. The server owns all of it, so everyone sees the same sky.

So far there is one day for the whole world, 48 minutes long and set by the wall clock (so a restart never jumps the sky, and players can learn it): 12 overcast, 12 rain, 8 overcast, 16 night. **Every third night is an aurora**: green light, the dead power lines hum and glow, copper wire turns up by the poles (finds that grow only then), watchers are restless. Only rain soaks you, so only rain falls on screen. Weather per region is still to come ([regional-weather](../roadmap/regional-weather.md)); so far a region's own weather is its storms (Hazards below).

**What the woods are like today.** The map never changes, only what happens on it, so the tenth trip is not the same as the first. Every day, from dawn (when the night ends), the Near Woods draws one condition, and half the days a second one, from a list in `content/items.json`; and one **weekly** condition everyone shares comes round in a fixed order, so nothing is missed for good. Weeks turn on Monday at 00:00 UTC, which is always a dawn. All of it follows the wall clock, like the weather, so everyone has the same day and a restart changes nothing. Conditions are made of four things only: **fog** (you see a few tiles past yourself), **finds** that grow only while the condition is on, the **watchers** waking only farther in (or closer, or not at all), and a shelter **fire going out** overnight (never the old cabin's).
**Built:** twelve daily conditions: thick fog (you see about 5 tiles), a NAPO cache the creek washed out of its bank (3 scrap and 3 wire by the pond, about 50 steps out, that do not grow back that day: first come, first served; nothing comes into the Zone from outside any more), the watchers moved north (they wake only 80 steps or more from home) or came closer (from 40), a shelter fire went out, glowcaps everywhere, the firs weeping resin, the cabin at the end opened (cloth), coffee in the shelters, flares in the old car, shards near the road (by the poles, 20 to 60 steps out), scrap by the old car. Two weekly ones: copper week (wire by every pole) and quiet woods (the watchers sleep). At dawn a banner and a soft chime say what today brings, Mira starts with the news ("Word from the woods today: thick fog, and a NAPO cache. This week: copper week."), and the notice board lists today's with what each means, this week's and next week's. The creek rising (the ford closed) waits for tiles that can close for a day.

## Hazards, anomalies and resistances

Like in Pacific Drive, the world itself wears you down, on top of the steady drain of being out there. Five elements, each with its own resistance:

| Element | Comes from (examples) |
|---|---|
| **Heat** | the burnt forest, summer afternoons, fire anomalies |
| **Cold** | night, winter, the snowy ridge, being wet in the wind |
| **Wind** | storms, open ridges and clearings |
| **Electricity** | electrical storms, flashes, broken power lines |
| **Radiation** | glowing anomalies, shards, the deepest places |

- **Anomalies are events**, shared by everyone on the map and announced before they hit so you can react: a **storm** rolls in over a region (wind, electricity, less to see); a **flash** marks a patch of ground that glows and then discharges; a **surge** that starts at the deepest place and rolls toward home. Being caught costs energy, less with the right resistance. Some anomalies leave rare finds behind.
- **Surges are built** (before the elements): each region can have a surge clock, fixed to the wall clock so everyone learns it ("the woods surge at a quarter to"). The Near Woods: every 40 minutes, 6 restless, then a surge of 2.5 minutes. Restless, rare finds show up deep in (shards, strange objects), and they are gone when the calm comes back: the best loot sits right before the danger. The surge's front starts at the deepest tile and reaches the way home in 90 seconds; wherever it has passed, away from a street light, energy drains 3 times as fast. Every phase is announced with a banner, and a clock shows in the status panel.
- **Storms and flashes are built.** Each region can have a storm clock, fixed to the wall clock like its surges. The Near Woods: every 40 minutes, halfway between two surges, a storm is announced a minute before and blows for 3 minutes. Out in it energy drains 1.5 times as fast (wind and electricity each cut half of the extra), it soaks you like rain, and being wet in its wind chills you half as much again (cold). The sky darkens, the fog closes in, lightning blinks. While it blows, shards turn up by the old poles. A flash starts every minute and a quarter near someone 30 steps or more out, often right under them: the ground glows for 8 seconds, faster and brighter, then discharges for 4 (a spark is electric, a fire flash hot); standing in it drains 6 times as fast. The status panel says what drains you, element by element.
- A shelter protects from everything outside. Resistance never makes you immune: it cuts the loss (a percentage per element).

## Equipment and stats

Six slots: **cap, shirt, gloves, pants, shoes, bag**. Better equipment means better resistances, so you can stay out longer and go where the weather or the anomalies would stop you.

- Each piece gives resistances to some elements (a raincoat: wind and cold; rubber gloves and boots: electricity; a lead-lined cap: radiation). Some also give a little extra energy.
- The **bag** decides how many slots you carry (a tote 6, a backpack 8, a hiking pack 12, an expedition pack 16).
- Gear comes in tiers (worn, sturdy, rugged, expedition, anomalous). It comes from **crafting at the workbench** (at home, beside the chest) with what you bring home, from rare finds deep in, and from trading with friends.
- Gear goes on and comes off at the chest at home, and out there too for a piece you carry (planned: [gear-on-the-road](../roadmap/gear-on-the-road.md)): the bag shows what you wear and how worn each piece is, a carried piece keeps its condition, quirk and upgrade, and what it replaces goes into the bag if there is room. The bag you wear changes only at home. Choosing what to wear and what to carry is part of planning a trip. What you wear is kept when you collapse; a piece in the bag falls into the pile like anything you carry. Your character wears what you equip, so others see your gear, unless you wear an outfit, which changes only how you look (planned: [outfits](../roadmap/outfits.md)).
- **A tap looks, a second step acts** ([gear-details](../roadmap/gear-details.md)): in the chest and the bag, a tap on a piece shows what it is (its tier, what it resists, the energy it adds, how worn it is, its quirk and level); a double tap, or the button in what the tap showed, puts it on or takes it off. The bag already works this way for everything else. **Built:** in the chest and at the workbench a tap on anything opens its card, and only its one button, A, or a second tap on the same thing within about a third of a second does something. A piece's card shows its drawing in its color, its name (with room for its level, "Raincoat +3"), its tier and the slot it goes in, what it resists and the energy it adds as worn as it is (with what it gives when mended), how worn it is as a bar and in words ("Like new", "Worn: 40% left", "Worn out: it protects nothing until it is mended"), and its quirk with what it does. Its button wears it, saying what goes into the stash in its place, or takes it off; the bag is never taken off (its card says how to change it), a bag too small for what you carry cannot be worn, and a worn-out piece says it can be mended at the workbench beside the chest. At the workbench a row opens the card of what it makes or mends, with what that takes against what the stash holds, and Make or Mend, which ask first in the text box ([ask-first](../roadmap/ask-first.md)); greyed out, they shake and the text box says what the stash lacks. Everything else in the chest opens its card too, with Put away or Take out. B closes the card first, then the panel. The card opens under the list in portrait and beside it in landscape, inside the panel. Gear in the bag waits for [gear-on-the-road](../roadmap/gear-on-the-road.md).
- **Upgrades, +1 to +9** (planned: [gear-upgrades](../roadmap/gear-upgrades.md)), at the workbench, with materials that grow rarer toward the top (shards from +4, strange objects for the last three). Each level makes a piece resist 5% more of what it resists and wear 5% more slowly. Up to +6 an upgrade always works; +7, +8 and +9 work 70%, 50% and 30% of the time, and a failure costs only the materials: the piece keeps its level, never breaks and never goes down. Worn clothes and bags are not upgraded. Resistances still stop at 75%.
- **Built:** everyone starts in worn clothes (they resist nothing) and a backpack (8 slots). The chest at home is where gear goes on and comes off: a tap on a piece you wear, or on gear in the stash, shows it, and its button (or a double tap) takes it off into the stash or puts it on (what it replaces goes into the stash); the bag can be changed, never taken off, and a smaller one has to hold what you carry. The **workbench at home** stands beside the chest by the fire, so making, mending and choosing gear happen in one room, from one stash; it makes gear from what your stash holds, into your stash (recipes are data: `recipes` in `content/items.json`). The lodge, where it used to stand, stacks its firewood there now. Sturdy gear takes cloth, resin, scrap and wire; rugged and expedition gear takes shards too; anomalous gear only comes out of strange objects. Cloth, which most recipes and every mend below anomalous need, grows by the wrecks, barrels and cabins of the Near Woods (four at a time, back in 5 to 10 minutes) and two to a room where rooms hold it: about 44 an hour for everyone, a raincoat in two good trips. Bags: a backpack 8, a hiking pack 12, an expedition pack 16. Some pieces add energy to the bar.
- **What resistances do so far** (the five elements are in Hazards below): **cold** softens the extra drain of rain, night and aurora and of being wet; **wind** slows how fast rain soaks you (a raincoat); **electricity** and **radiation** each cut half of a surge's extra drain, so full protection takes both; **wind** and **electricity** each cut half of a storm's extra drain; **heat** cuts a fire flash's, **electricity** a spark's (welding gloves are the first heat gear). Resistances add up over what you wear and stop at 75%.
- **Gear wears out, and rare gear has quirks (built).** Each piece has a condition. Out in the wilds, what you wear wears down: sturdy gear in 90 minutes out there, rugged in 2 hours, expedition in 2.5, anomalous in 3 (worn clothes and bags never do). Below a quarter left it protects less and less, and gives less energy; worn out, nothing, until it is mended at the workbench (sturdy: 2 cloth and 1 scrap; rugged: 3 and 2; expedition: 4 and 3; anomalous: 2 scrap and a shard), which makes it whole again. A piece keeps its condition in the stash; the chest shows each piece on its own, and you pick which one to put on. Gear cannot be taken out of the chest (gear a strange object turns into waits in the bag, not yet a piece, until you stash it), so a worn piece never comes back new. Anomalous pieces have a quirk, rolled when one first lands in your stash or on you: **glowing steps** (out there your footprints glow for half a minute, for everyone to follow), **restless light** (street lights flicker as you pass, for everyone to see) and **humming** (a minute before your region grows restless, before anyone is told). The Wearing row and the stash show how much is left of each piece, and the Status panel what is wearing down and your quirks.

## Items

Everything is data (`content/items.json`: name, what it is, stack size, stats, where it grows), so the list can grow and be tuned without code. A first set:

- **Resources** (for crafting and XP): glowcaps (they paint marks), fir resin and cloth scraps (they burn), scrap metal (heavy), copper wire, anomaly shards (rare, deep in; the Old Stone wants them).
- **Consumables** (used from the bag): a thermos (+30 energy), a road flare (45 seconds of red light: creatures keep off, a hitchhiker lets go), hand warmers (cold resistance for a while), rad tablets (radiation resistance for a while).
- **Strange objects**, found deep in (more while a region is restless): what they are shows only when you look at them in town, in the light (a house in town counts). Mostly shards, a thermos or flares; now and then a charm.
- **Charms** work while they are in your bag, one of each kind: a warm pebble (rain soaks you 40% slower), a hollow feather (your bag feels 25% lighter), a humming bead (hitchhikers find you 60% less often).
- **Live finds** turn up only while a region is restless, and fade on the way home. **Built:** the three shards that grow deep in the Near Woods while it is restless are **live shards**. One is worth 40 XP stashed within 4 minutes of picking it up, then 5 XP less a minute until, at 10 minutes, it is a plain anomaly shard (12 XP). The clock runs on the wall clock, so it fades while you are offline too. While you carry one, a pale column of light stands over you that everyone on the map sees from beyond the fog, and watchers come for you from farther. Put down (dropped, lost to a watcher, or in the pile when you collapse) it goes dim for good; the Old Stone takes it like any shard. The bag shows a ring that empties and "Worth 40 XP for 3:12 more", then "Fading: 30 XP now"; picking one up says "It is still live". How long it stays fresh is data (`live` in `content/items.json`), to tune after play-testing.
- **A NAPO lockbox** (planned: [daily-parcels](../roadmap/daily-parcels.md)), sealed since the evacuation, comes in Sunday's parcel to whoever played all week, and is opened at the chest: shards, a strange object, a charm, or a good stack of cloth and wire.
- Every item has a **weight** (it shows in the bag with what burns and what a charm does).
- **Equipment**: a few pieces per slot and tier, starting with worn clothes everyone has.
- **Tools: made at the workbench or found, then in the bag's header for good.** Each is a button there (every paper map is the one map button), in the order you got them. A tool is never used up, takes no slot, weighs nothing, never goes into a pile, the stash or a trade, and nothing takes it away. Making one asks first like anything made ("Make a radio? It uses 2 wire and 1 scrap."), and the box then says where it went: "You make a radio. It is yours for good: its button is in your bag." Picked up, one floats over your head like any find ("+ Radio"). You own each one once: at the workbench its card says "You have it", its button greyed (pressed, the box says why), and a find of one you have stays where it lies, for someone else. Tapping one says what it is; what each does comes with it: the radio ([crackle-radio](../roadmap/crackle-radio.md)), the bolt cutters and waders ([locked-places](../roadmap/locked-places.md)), the maps found farther out ([far-woods](../roadmap/far-woods.md), [inked-maps](../roadmap/inked-maps.md)). **Built:** each player's tools are saved with them; so far everyone's are the three paper maps they start with.

## Status

A status tab (in the menu, and a tap on the status panel top left opens it) shows your level and XP, energy (maximum, now, and what is draining it at the moment, element by element), your five resistances with where they come from (each piece of gear), and any effects running (a hand warmer, a storm you are in).

Built so far: energy, how wet you are, your load, what clings to you, your charms, the surge, the Old Stone, and your **feats**. Feats are small perks for good, earned by what you do out there, not bought, and each climbs five ranks as what counts toward it adds up: the first comes in the first hours, the last after months of playing, never from grinding (like The Long Dark's skills).

**Built:** seven feats, five ranks each, every rank a little stronger than the one before:

- **Rain walker** (steps out in the rain): rain soaks you 20, 30, 40, 45 and 50% slower, at 1,500, 5,000, 15,000, 40,000 and 100,000 steps.
- **Night owl** (steps out in the dark): hitchhikers find you 50, 60, 70, 75 and 80% less often, at 1,000, 3,500, 10,000, 25,000 and 60,000 steps.
- **Pack mule** (steps out with a heavy bag): what you carry feels 15, 20, 25, 28 and 30% lighter, at 800, 2,500, 8,000, 20,000 and 50,000 steps.
- **Fire keeper** (fires fed): fires warm you 15, 20, 25, 28 and 30% faster, at 20, 60, 200, 500 and 1,200 fires.
- **Mender** (pieces mended at the workbench): gear wears 5, 10, 15, 20 and 25% slower out there, at 5, 15, 40, 100 and 250 pieces.
- **Forager** (finds picked up out in the wilds, the shelters out there included; not piles, and not in town): a find comes up double 5, 8, 11, 13 and 15% of the time, at 200, 700, 2,000, 5,000 and 12,000 finds. Never a live find, and the second one only if the bag has room for it.
- **Pathfinder** (steps out in the wilds 85 or more walking steps from home): that far out, and only there, the drain is 3, 6, 9, 12 and 15% gentler, at 500, 1,500, 5,000, 12,000 and 30,000 steps.

Rank 1 of the first four is what those feats were before they had ranks, at the same counts, and what was counted before carries over: whoever had counted more already is at that rank when they come back, without a banner. A new rank is announced once, with a banner ("Rain walker, rank 2" and "Rain soaks you 30% slower."), as soon as no panel, text box or other banner is in the way. The status panel shows each feat with its rank as five pips, what the rank does (before rank 1, what rank 1 will do), and a bar with how far the next rank is ("3,212 of 5,000 steps in the rain to rank 2"); at rank 5, "Top rank".

Your level shows next to your name, and the Status panel starts with your level and the XP to the next one; a guest's starts with what playing as a guest means, and the Sign in button ([Play first, sign in to keep it](#together-chat-and-friends)).

## Together: chat and friends

- **World chat** for everyone online, and **local chat** for whoever is near you: it shows as a speech bubble over your head (FireRed style) and in the chat log. Reached from the chat button next to the menu (Enter on a keyboard), which shows a dot when there is something new.
  **Built:** the chat button beside the menu opens it (Enter opens it with its line ready to type in), with a Near you tab (your map, 12 tiles around you; a bubble over the speaker's head for 6 seconds) and an Everyone tab. Only signed-in players talk: a guest hears what is said, and where the line to type in would be, finds one card that says signing in lets them chat, with the Sign in button. 120 characters a message, 5 every 10 seconds. Words on the list (`content/words.json`) are masked with asterisks, however they are spelled. Nothing said is kept or logged; your game keeps the last 100 lines until you close it. Tapping a name opens that player's card, to befriend, block (you no longer hear them) or report them (with the line they wrote).
- **Friends**: send a request (tap a player, or by name), accept or decline; the friends list shows who is online and where they are. **Private messages** between friends, kept until read.
  **Built:** Friends in the menu (a dot there when someone asked you or wrote you): ask by name, answer requests, see friends online and on which map, open someone's card to write to them, unfriend, block or report them. Tapping a player's name tag opens their card. Friends need sign-in on both sides: a guest opening Friends (or someone's card) finds one card that says so, with the Sign in button, and a guest's card says they play as a guest instead of offering "Ask to be friends"; a guest can still be blocked and reported, and cannot report until they sign in, since a report needs someone who answers for it. Messages are deleted from the server once read, so a conversation lasts only as long as the session. Blocking ends a friendship and keeps them from asking or writing again; whoever is blocked hears only that you take no requests. Reports are kept for the maintainers with what was written, as the reporter quoted it. A setting turns friend requests off. Limits: 200 characters a message, 20 a minute, 50 unread from one friend, 20 requests waiting. The word filter comes with chat.
- **Exchange, face to face only**: two friends on the same map, at most **10 tiles** apart, open a trade: each puts in items or gear, both confirm the final offer, and the server swaps them in one step (nothing can go missing halfway). Walking farther apart cancels it.
- **Settings** (in the menu): anyone can turn off friend requests and trade requests.
- Safety from day one: limits on length and speed, a word filter, block and report.
- **Play first, sign in to keep it** ([play-first](../roadmap/play-first.md)): a first visit should end in the woods, not at an email form. A first-time visitor plays at once, as a guest whose character lives in that browser. Chat, friends, trading, the daily parcels and outfits need sign-in, since chat, friends and items are worth keeping safe; the moment it is worth it to them, signing in keeps everything.
  **Built:** the first card is the game's name, its one line, a name and a big **Play now**; "I have played before: sign in" is the quiet link under it. The name makes a **guest**: a character like the ones made before sign-in, whose login token lives in that browser (the server keeps only a scrambled copy) and nothing says who plays it. That browser comes straight back in as its guest, no card. Top left, under the name, the status panel says **Guest** with a **Sign in** button, and the Status tab (a tap on the panel opens it) says it plainly: a guest's progress lives in this browser and is lost with its data, a guest who stays away for 30 days is deleted (with their pile and marks; never before the rule has been in the game for 30 days, so characters from before sign-in, which count as guests, get a month's notice), and signing in keeps everything. Sign in, from there or from the menu, and the character becomes the account's (the claim below): "Signed in. Your character is yours to keep." An account keeps one character: if it has one already, it stays the account's, and the sign-in card says so before anything changes ("This account already has a character, Aldo. Play as Aldo? Your guest character stays in this browser."), with the way back to the guest; signing out brings the guest back. What a guest cannot use yet is never greyed out without a word: the chat and the friends panel each show one card that says what signing in opens, with the button. Signing in is **an email and a one-time code, through Supabase Auth**; Google and Apple come later, through the same sign-in ([sign-in-google-apple](../roadmap/sign-in-google-apple.md)). The game server checks the Supabase login and keeps its own players and game data in its own database; a character made without sign-in (a guest, or one from before sign-in), which lives only in the browser that made it, is claimed by signing in on that browser, and from then on its token alone plays it no more.

## Look and feel

- Low-poly 3D with the structure of Pokemon FireRed: a steep, almost top-down camera over a tile grid, ledges and walls of trees, chunky chibi characters, two-tone toon shading with outlines, a text box at the bottom for talking.
- A **dark world** in the mood of **Pacific Drive**: a Pacific Northwest forest of tall firs, fog, rain, night, abandoned cabins, orange sodium street lights, utility poles, glowing anomalies (the Old Stone, wisps), and the old facilities, antennas and warning signs NAPO left behind ([the story](#the-story-napo-and-napoland)).
- **NAPO's things look like NAPO's:** concrete buildings with flat roofs rimmed in NAPO yellow (concrete inside too, not logs), yellow warning signs edged in black, desks whose screens still glow green, and the Tower, a red and white mast with a dish turned toward the woods and a red light that blinks day and night. Each townsperson has colors of their own; NAPO's old lineman still wears his yellow hard hat.
- **Outfits** (planned: [outfits](../roadmap/outfits.md)): how your character looks, whatever gear it wears, with no stats. Everyone starts in the same one, given with the first sign-in: a NAPO work suit from the station's stores. More come with levels (the lineman's jacket at 5, the survey rain cape at 10, the ranger's coat at 15, the residents' patchwork at 20). Past level 20, merits buy jacket patterns and name tag badges ([past-twenty](../roadmap/past-twenty.md)), and later a shop sells looks only, never power, and never one that can be earned ([looks-shop](../roadmap/looks-shop.md)). The pack you carry still shows over any outfit, and your color (arrows, name) stays yours.
- **Grass that looks like grass.** **Built:** on every map the ground's color drifts the way a meadow's does, paler where it is dry, deeper where it is damp, dark and olive under the edge of the trees, a touch different from tile to tile, and always the same in the same place; the old asphalt, the paths and the mud vary too. Low tufts grow on the grass, and the tall grass (Creatures) sways, harder in a storm, and parts around whoever wades into it. The rest of [richer-places](../roadmap/richer-places.md), more of the story in the places, is still to come.
- Dark interface: dark panels with cream borders, Fredoka and Nunito fonts.
- **Sound** carries the mood and the warnings, so a thumb on the stick does not have to read to stay alive. **Built:** every sound is made in the browser (Web Audio), nothing is downloaded. Rain outdoors (faint through a roof), wind in the wilds and harder in a storm, a fire that crackles loud when fed and quieter as it burns low and as you walk away, footsteps that change on road, grass and ferns, mud, water and plank floors, with a swish through tall grass (only yours), and the power lines humming near the poles on aurora nights. Warnings: a low bell when a region grows restless and a low tone while it stays so, a rumble that rises as the surge front comes toward you and a hard drone once it is on you, a rising tone when a storm is coming, thunder a moment after each lightning blink, a crackle when a flash starts near you and a pop when it bursts, a dragging sound while a watcher moves, gone the moment it freezes, and ferns thrashing while a skulker chases anyone near you, with a sharp cry the moment one goes after you. The menu has a Sound row: mute and volume (on at 70% to start), kept by this browser.
- **Any screen shape works:** nothing forces portrait or landscape. The camera shows the same circle of world around you on every screen; controls sit in the thumb corners and are sized from the short side; panels open where they cover the least.

## References

Games we learn from when designing what comes next:

- **Pacific Drive** (Ironwood Studios): the mood (a dark Pacific Northwest forest, rain, abandoned things, glowing anomalies), and the loop of leaving a safe place, pushing your luck out there and coming back changed. Anomalies and storms that wear you down.
- **Pokémon FireRed** (Game Freak): the structure: a tile grid under a steep camera, towns and routes as separate maps, every building enterable, a text box for talking, controls that stay simple.
- **The Long Dark** (Hinterland Studio): survival as quiet pressure: cold, wind and exposure, fire as the thing that keeps you going, cabins to scavenge and shelter in, and maps drawn by hand that never show where you are, so you learn the land by its landmarks.
